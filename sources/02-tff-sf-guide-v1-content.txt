0. The 2-Week Scout Trip (from India)
Stage: Deciding · Type: Guide · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: A playbook for a 14-day B-1 trip to the Bay Area before you
commit to moving. You’ll leave with a visa plan, a meeting-dense itinerary, and a clear
go/no-go answer.

What a B-1 lets you do (and what it doesn’t)
Jargon: B-1 (the US “business visitor” visa — lets you attend meetings, not take a job).
Most Indian founders get a combined B-1/B-2 stamp.
On a B-1 you can: meet investors and pitch them, negotiate contracts, attend
conferences and demo days, consult with business associates, scout offices, open
conversations with lawyers and accountants. The State Department’s own fact sheet
explicitly lists soliciting investment and negotiating contracts as allowable.
You cannot: take US-source salary, do billable work for a US entity, freelance, or “run” a
US operation day-to-day. Building your own product on your laptop in a cafe sits in a
grey zone; getting paid by anyone in the US does not — it’s a clear no. Don’t tell the CBP
officer at the airport you’re “moving to SF.” You’re visiting for meetings and conferences,
which is the truth for this trip.

Fix the visa timeline first
B-1/B-2 interview wait times in India are the single biggest constraint. As of early 2026,
first-time interview waits were roughly: Chennai ~1–2 months, Kolkata ~2–3
months, Delhi ~6–8 months, Hyderabad ~7–8 months, Mumbai ~9–10 months.
Check live numbers on the State Department’s Global Visa Wait Times page — they
move monthly.
Do this: pay the MRV fee once, book the earliest slot anywhere in India (a flight to
Chennai beats a 9-month Mumbai wait), then keep rescheduling — the portal releases
cancellations daily and rescheduling is free. If you already hold a valid B-1/B-2, skip this
section and book flights.

Build a meeting-dense fortnight
Aim for 4–6 meetings or events per day. The structure that works:
•​   Days 1–2: land, get a transit card, walk SoMa/Hayes Valley/Mission. Message every
     contact: “In SF till the 14th — 30 minutes for coffee?”
•​   Days 3–9: the anchor event week. Register on Luma for everything relevant (SF
     Tech Week runs its calendar on luma.com/sftw). Prioritise small dinners and
     20-person meetups over 500-person panels — that’s where second-degree intros
     happen.
•​   Days 10–12: 1:1s generated by the week. Ask every person the same two
     questions: “Who else should I meet?” and “Would you use/fund this?”
•​   Days 13–14: Peninsula/South Bay day trip (Caltrain), then write your go/no-go
     memo before you fly home.
Book meetings from India, 2–3 weeks out. Cold outreach converts far better with “I’m
in SF Oct 3–17” in the first line.

What to validate before committing
You are not here to raise on this trip. You’re here to answer five questions in writing:
1.​ Did 10+ relevant people (customers, investors, operators) take a meeting?
2.​ Does your pitch land differently here — and how did it need to change?
3.​ Can you see yourself living here at SF prices (walk a few neighbourhoods, price a
    room)?
4.​ Did you find at least one community you’d plug into (Indian founder groups, sector
    meetups)?
5.​ Did anyone say “tell me when you’re back” — real pull, not politeness?
Three or more clear yeses: start the move checklist. Otherwise, run the wait/don’t-move
logic in the Reality Check guide.

2. Start With Your Decision
2. Start With Your Decision
Stage: All · Type: Index · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: The 10 decisions that define your move, each as a card: the
question, what it turns on, and which guide to read. Work top to bottom — later
decisions depend on earlier ones.

D1 — Go, wait, or don’t?
The question: Should you move at all, and now? It turns on five things: 12+ months
runway at SF burn, a US-weighted market, a lawyer-validated visa path, 3+ year
commitment, and demonstrated pull from US customers or investors. Three or fewer
yeses means wait — and “wait” is a strategy with a to-do list, not a rejection. Read:
Reality Check (03), Scout Trip (00)

D2 — Which visa path?
The question: B-1 scout now — then O-1, H-1B, or L-1? It turns on your evidence
profile and your entity. The O-1 (extraordinary-ability visa: no cap, no lottery, needs 3 of
8 criteria — funding, press, awards, judging) is the founder default in 2026. H-1B is a
lottery with a wage-weighted system that penalises modest founder salaries. L-1A
works if you’ve run an Indian entity 1+ years and open a US office. Start the O-1
evidence folder regardless — it’s also your fundraising credibility file. Read: Visa Paths,
Scout Trip (00)

D3 — Entity: flip, fresh, or subsidiary?
The question: Delaware flip (US parent, Indian company becomes subsidiary), fresh US
incorporation, or a US subsidiary of your Indian parent? It turns on where your
investors and revenue will sit. US VCs overwhelmingly want a Delaware C-Corp parent.
But the flip is expensive to reverse — reverse-flipping later can trigger heavy US
capital-gains tax (Meesho’s cost ran into hundreds of millions) — so don’t flip on
speculation. Fresh incorporation is cleanest if the Indian entity has little history;
subsidiary keeps optionality if India remains your base. Jargon: Flip (restructuring so a
new US company owns your Indian company). Read: Entity Decision, Remote Setup (14)

D4 — Trip length: scout, sprint, or permanent?
The question: 2-week scout, 3-month sprint (B-1 max stay is typically 6 months), or the
one-way move? It turns on how much you’ve validated. No US pull yet → scout.
Validated pull but visa pending → sprint(s) while the O-1 files. Visa in hand and D1 says
go → permanent. Skipping straight to permanent without a scout is how people
discover SF prices and isolation simultaneously. Read: Scout Trip (00), Reality Check (03)

D5 — Solo first, or family together?
The question: Move alone and set up, or land together? It turns on housing and schools.
Solo-first (4–8 weeks) lets you pick an area, secure a lease and absorb the chaos before
your family lands — but doubles housing costs briefly and delays their settling.
Family-together works when kids’ school-year timing forces it; then you must nail the
area decision faster. Spouse work rights depend on your visa type — check before
deciding. Read: Family Logistics, Where to Live

D6 — SF vs Peninsula vs South Bay vs East Bay?
The question: Where do you actually live? It turns on your daily graph. SF proper:
highest events/investor density, highest rent (~$4,000+ median 1BR, mid-2026), best
for pre-seed networking. Peninsula (Palo Alto–Redwood City): VC proximity, quieter,
car-ish. South Bay: cheaper-ish, enterprise/hardware gravity, car mandatory. East Bay:
best value, real commute. Decide from the ground in month 1 — never from India. Read:
Where to Live, Housing

D7 — Short-term stay vs lease?
The question: When do you sign a 12-month lease? It turns on the area decision. Book
weeks 1–4 furnished (co-living, hacker house, monthly sublet), decide your area, then
lease. Leases need proof of income or a fat deposit, and your rental resume (documents
packet: funds proof, India landlord references, company story) does the work your
missing US credit score can’t. Read: Housing, Remote Setup (14)

D8 — Keep Indian number and banking, or cut over?
The question: How much of your Indian financial identity do you keep alive? Keep the
number, always — Indian bank OTPs, UPI and Aadhaar services die without it. Run dual
SIM: US eSIM primary, Indian SIM on roaming for SMS. Banking: keep accounts (convert
to NRO/NRE as your residency status changes — talk to your bank), keep one Indian
card active. Cutting over fully is a year-three decision, not a week-one one. Read: Remote
Setup (14), Credit & Banking

D9 — Travel insurance vs marketplace plan?
The question: How are you covered, and when do you switch? It’s a sequence, not a
choice: travel insurance covers days 0–60; Covered California’s special-enrolment
window for new residents closes 60 days after your move — enrol inside it or risk
months uninsured at $1,500–3,500 per uninsured ER visit. Unsubsidised premiums
average ~$728/month in 2026; budget it like rent. Read: Health Insurance

D10 — Corridor VCs, US-native funds, or angels?
The question: Who do you pitch first? It turns on your story’s legibility. Corridor funds
(US–India cross-border investors who know both markets) price Indian traction
correctly and move on thinner US proof. US-native funds pay better prices but
pattern-match on networks you’re still building — they’re your second wave. Operator
angels (often Indian-origin) are the fastest first cheques and the intro engine to both.
Map all three before you pitch any. Read: Fundraising, Community

3. Reality Check + Who Should NOT Move
Stage: Deciding · Type: Guide · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: The honest case against moving, so you decide with open eyes.
Read this before booking anything; finish with the 5-question self-assessment.

The five costs nobody puts in the pitch
1. Visa uncertainty is a permanent tax. There is no clean “founder visa.” Your realistic
paths — O-1, H-1B, L-1 — all involve lawyers, evidence-gathering, waiting, and the
possibility of a no. Founders describe the constant sense of being at the mercy of an
anonymous official; it shapes hiring, travel, and fundraising for years. Every plan you
make gets a visa asterisk.
2. SF is at record-high cost. As of mid-2026, the median SF one-bedroom is around
$4,000–4,060/month per Zumper — the highest in the site’s decade-plus of data,
up ~22% year-over-year, driven by AI hiring and a near-empty construction pipeline.
Add ~$150–300/mo utilities and internet, $15 lunches, and you’re at
$6,000–7,500/month ($72,000–90,000/year) for a modest solo life in the city. A
room in a shared flat cuts rent to ~$1,500–2,500 but doesn’t change the ambient cost of
everything else.
3. The healthcare gap is real money. India trained you to treat healthcare as cheap
and on-demand. In the US, an unsubsidised individual marketplace plan averages
~$728/month in California (2026), expanded federal subsidies expired for 2026, and
an uninsured ER visit runs $1,500–3,500 for a non-critical case — five figures if
anything serious happens. Until your income is US-verifiable, subsidy math is messy.
Budget insurance like rent, not like an afterthought.
4. Isolation hits at month three. The first month is adrenaline and coffee meetings.
Month three is when the time-zone gap eats your family calls (India is 12.5 hours
ahead), your social graph is still transactional, and everyone you meet is also too busy.
Founders who thrive here joined one recurring community early; founders who quietly
went home usually didn’t.
5. Fundraising as an outsider is slower than the podcast said. US-native VCs
pattern-match on networks you don’t have yet. Warm-intro culture means your first
quarter is spent building the graph, not pitching. Corridor funds (India-US cross-border
investors) close the gap but bring their own pricing. Being in SF improves your odds; it
does not suspend gravity.

The go / wait / don’t self-assessment
Answer honestly, in writing:
1.​ Runway: After move costs, do we have 12+ months of cash at SF burn?
2.​ Market: Are our next 100 customers (or our investors) primarily in the US?

first. 0–2 yes → don’t move now; build from India and revisit in six months. “Wait” is a
strategy, not a failure.

Jargon: A petition is the application a US company files with USCIS asking to employ
you; you can’t file for yourself directly, but a company you own can petition for you.

B-1: scout, don’t work
The B-1 lets you take meetings, negotiate, and talk to investors. It does not let you write
production code for your US entity, sign as a US employee, or draw US salary. Border
officers have tightened scrutiny of B-1 entries since 2025 — carry a return ticket, a
meeting schedule, and proof of Indian ties.
The real problem is the interview queue. First-time B1/B2 interview waits at Indian
consulates ran roughly 100–400 days across 2025–26 depending on city; Chennai and
Hyderabad have often been shorter than Mumbai and Delhi. If you already hold a valid
B1/B2 from a past trip, you’ve dodged this entirely. Check current waits on the State
Department’s per-city tool before planning — these numbers move monthly (needs
verification at time of reading).

O-1A: the founder default
The O-1A is for “extraordinary ability” in business or STEM. That sounds intimidating; in
practice it’s an evidence-assembly exercise. You must hit 3 of 8 USCIS criteria (codified
at 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii)):
1.​ Awards · 2. Selective memberships · 3. Press about you · 4. Judging others’ work · 5.
    Original contributions of major significance · 6. Scholarly articles · 7. Critical role at
    distinguished organizations · 8. High remuneration.
Funded founders most commonly clear: critical role (founder/CTO of a VC-backed
company), high remuneration (Indian salary benchmarked against Indian norms
counts), judging (hackathon/accelerator judging, peer review), press (named coverage
in real publications), and original contributions (patents, widely adopted product). VC
funding from recognized investors helps under awards/critical role. Start collecting
evidence months before you engage a lawyer.
Numbers as of mid-2026 (verify current): - Approval rate: ~94% recently; RFE rate
~18.7%. Jargon: An RFE (Request for Evidence) is USCIS asking for more proof — it
adds 2–4 months but usually still ends in approval. - Costs: attorney $6,000–15,000;
government filing ~$780–1,380; premium processing $2,965 (rate effective March 1,
2026) for a 15-business-day decision. All-in: $12–18k. - Timeline: petition prep 4–8 weeks, then 15 business
days with premium processing, then consulate stamping in India. 3–4 months end-to-end if your evidence is
ready. - Duration: 3 years initially, extendable indefinitely in 1-year increments. Spouses
get O-3 (no work authorization — plan for this).
Why O-1A beats waiting on H-1B: no lottery, no annual cap, and critically no
per-country cap — the thing that puts India-born applicants in decade-long
employment green-card queues doesn’t apply here.

H-1B transfer: only if you already have one
If you’re on H-1B in the US or held one recently, your Delaware C-corp can file a transfer
— no new lottery. USCIS requires an employer-employee relationship, so your board
(not you) must control your employment. Doable with investors on the board; awkward
as a solo founder with no outside directors.

Think green card from day one
The O-1A is temporary. The long-term play is EB-1A (the green-card cousin of O-1A,
with a higher evidence bar). EB-1 India is backlogged: the July 2026 Final Action Date
sits around December 2022, and realistic I-140-to-green-card timelines for India run
~4–6 years. File your I-140 as early as your evidence supports it — filing locks your
priority date (your place in the queue), and every month you delay is a month added at
the back end.

The decision rule: US VC fundraising forces Delaware — most US funds and YC won’t

The Indian-side rules: ODI 2022 and round-tripping
The Overseas Investment (OI) Rules, 2022 (issued August 2022) govern Indians
acquiring foreign shares. Two things matter for a flip:
•​   Round-tripping is now permitted for bona fide business — the old blanket ban
     on structures where a foreign entity owns an Indian one (money “rounding” back to
     India) is gone, but only up to two layers of subsidiaries and only where the US
     parent has genuine substance: real US customers, US operations, a documented
     commercial rationale. A shell Delaware parent existing purely to hold Indian shares
     invites RBI trouble.
•​   Your share swap uses your LRS headroom. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme
     caps each resident Indian at $250,000 per financial year for overseas investment.
     Acquiring US parent shares (by swap or purchase) is an ODI transaction under
     these rules, valued by a SEBI-registered merchant banker. Budget the valuation
     report at ₹1–2 lakh and FEMA/RBI filings (Form FC, annual APR) at ₹1–3 lakh in
     professional fees.
Jargon: FEMA is India’s Foreign Exchange Management Act — the law behind all of this.
ODI is Overseas Direct Investment — an Indian resident buying into a foreign company.

8. Healthcare & Insurance
Stage: Month 1 · Type: Guide · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: How to get health coverage in your first 60 days in the Bay Area
— the single most missable deadline of your entire move — and how the US system
actually works when you use it.

The 60-day clock starts when you land
The US has no NHS, no CGHS, no “just walk into Apollo.” Coverage comes from an
employer plan or one you buy yourself. If your startup doesn’t have a group plan yet
(most pre-seed startups don’t), you buy through Covered California, the state’s health
insurance marketplace.
Here’s the deadline: moving to California is a Qualifying Life Event (QLE) — a life
change that lets you enroll outside the normal open-enrollment window
(November–January). You get 60 days from your move date to enroll in a Covered
California plan. Miss it, and you may wait months for the next open enrollment —
uninsured the whole time.
Jargon: Qualifying Life Event (QLE) — a change (move, marriage, job loss, birth) that
unlocks a special enrollment window.
Do this in week 1: create a Covered California account, have proof of your move ready
(lease agreement or a utility bill), and enroll. Lawfully present immigrants — H-1B, O-1,
L-1, and their dependents — are eligible to buy marketplace plans.

The bridge: your first few weeks
Marketplace coverage typically starts the 1st of the month after you enroll. That leaves a
gap between landing and coverage start. Cover it with new-immigrant / visitor
medical insurance — short-term travel medical plans sold by providers like Insubuy or
VisitorsCoverage. As of 2026 these run roughly $20–170/month depending on age and
coverage level. They cover emergencies, not routine care or pre-existing conditions. Buy
before you fly; it’s cheaper and you’re covered from wheels-down.

Start with the persona, not the neighborhood
The SF-versus-everywhere-else debate resolves fast once you're honest about your daily
graph: where your co-founder, your customers, and your weeknight events actually are.
Pick the persona first.

Solo founder building an AI or software startup

San Francisco city, and it isn't close. Your investors, hiring pool, and every weeknight
event are here. Hayes Valley (dense with AI founders, walk everywhere), the Mission
(SF's sunniest weather, food, younger crowd; averaging just under $4,000 a month as of

block; see scams and safety below). The western fog belt (Sunset, Richmond) was the
reliable discount, about $1,300 under the citywide median in early-2026 data, but the
boom has spilled west: Zumper had Outer Sunset one-bedrooms averaging $3,395 by

survives, just thinner and noisier than its reputation, and you're still cold in July and
30–40 minutes from everything. For SF-specific extras beyond housing (the events and
community layer), Michelle Fang's Starter to SF wiki is the community reference, and
there's no point rebuilding what she maintains.

Family, or a life that needs a car

South Bay and Fremont. Fremont, Sunnyvale, Santa Clara, and Cupertino are the actual
desi hubs: Fremont has roughly 69,000 Indian residents and San Jose 79,000, the two
largest Indian populations of any California city. In practice that means Indian groceries
on the main roads, temples, and schools full of kids who look like yours. You will not feel
like an outsider here, and you'll pay $1,500–1,900 a month less than SF for more space.
If schools are the deciding variable, the family logistics guide covers districts and the
Tri-Valley option.

Fundraising-heavy founder who doesn't want the city

The Peninsula. San Mateo and Redwood City sit on Caltrain between SF and Palo Alto —
investor proximity, quieter streets, functioning downtowns. It's a compromise at full
price: one-bedrooms run $2,800–3,700 depending on the source and the building's age.
I'd pick it only if your calendar genuinely splits between Sand Hill Road and SF;
otherwise it's Peninsula prices for neither city's upside.

Tight budget, SF access still required

East Bay. Oakland and Berkeley one-bedrooms run $2,000–2,300, roughly half of SF, and
BART gets you from the central stations to downtown SF in about 20–30 minutes (the
Getting Around guide has the full transport math). Rockridge and Temescal for walkable
cafe life, downtown Oakland for pure value. Fremont technically sits in the East Bay too
and doubles as the budget desi option.

Neighborhood shortlists

San Francisco. Hayes Valley is the default first-year-founder pick: RentCafe's all-unit

East Bay. Rockridge and Temescal are the walkable picks (College and Telegraph

The cash to land
Bangalore trained you to budget ten months' deposit. California went the other way.
Since July 2024, state law caps security deposits at one month's rent for most landlords
(AB 12, amending Civil Code 1950.5); individuals who own at most two rental
properties totalling four units may still ask for two months. "First, last, and deposit" is
dead too — last month's rent counts toward the same cap.

So move-in on a $4,000 SF one-bedroom is: first month $4,000, deposit $4,000, an
application fee capped statewide at $65.86 in 2026 (Berkeley posts $68.96), and renters
insurance if the lease requires it (most big buildings do) at roughly $13–25 a month in
California. A hair over $8,000 to get keys, before furniture. Land with three months of
total costs in an accessible account and no landlord can pressure you into a bad lease
with a deadline.

When you leave, the deposit must come back within 21 days, itemized. And note the
quiet good news threaded through all of this: every protection on this page attaches to
the tenancy, not the tenant. Your visa status is irrelevant to the deposit cap, the rent
caps, and the eviction rules.


Renting with zero US credit history
Jargon: credit score — a US lending-history number (300–850) most landlords screen
for, alongside income verification at 2.5–3x the monthly rent. On arrival you have
neither the score nor, often, the US income. Three doors still open.

Before any of them: build a rental resume. One PDF with your visa status, funding
announcement or bank balance, LinkedIn, a reference letter from your Indian landlord,
and what you're offering as security. Send it with every enquiry — you're marketing
yourself past a missing number. The documents folder in the remote setup guide has
you collecting these ingredients before you fly.

Door one: small landlords. Owners of two-to-four-unit buildings decide with judgment,
not screening software. The rental resume, a real conversation, and the full one-month
deposit routinely close them, and they surface on Craigslist and neighborhood walks
more than on the big listing sites.

Door two: a guarantor service, for the corporate towers. Large property managers won't
bend their software, but most accept TheGuarantors, which insures your lease so you
qualify without US credit. It explicitly serves international arrivals, one policy covers the
whole lease, and it takes wires from non-US banks. The one-time premium is priced
after underwriting; 2026 comparisons put typical pricing at roughly 40–130% of one
month's rent, and no-US-credit profiles land toward the top of that band. Get the quote
before you tour, so approval takes hours instead of days.

Door three: prepay six months. The deposit cap has exactly one pressure valve written
into it — a landlord may accept an advance payment of six months' rent or more, if the
lease runs six months or longer. Six or more is the whole rule: below that there is no
middle option, and two or three months upfront is just an over-cap deposit the landlord
shouldn't take. If you have the cash and want a specific unit, "six months wired at
signing" ends most credit conversations on the spot.

Two footnotes. A landlord runs your credit themselves for that $66 fee — anyone who
instead sends you a link to buy your own credit report is running a scam, not a
screening. And building the actual US score starts the week you land, before the SSN
arrives; that whole sequence is in the money and credit guide.


Rent control in 60 seconds
This is where the old-versus-new building choice turns into money. Three tiers:

   ●​ SF, multi-unit building with a certificate of occupancy before June 13, 1979:
      rent-controlled. The allowable increase from March 1, 2026 through February 28,
      2027 is 1.6%, and eviction protections are strong.
   ●​ Newer SF buildings and the rest of the Bay, when the building is older than about
      15 years: the statewide cap of 5% plus regional CPI. That's 6.3% through July 31,
      2026, then it jumps to 8.8% on August 1 — days after this page was written.
   ●​ Buildings under 15 years old, and most single-family homes owned by
      individuals: no cap at all.

Run year two before you sign. A $3,800 pre-1979 SF unit can rise $61 a month. A $3,500
newer build in Sunnyvale can legally rise $308, and in this market it will. I'd take the
older building at slightly higher starting rent almost every time — your rent barely
moves in years two through five while market rent climbs 20%+. Check any SF
building's construction year before touring, and confirm a specific unit's status with the
SF Rent Board at (415) 252-4602; the official increase rules are here.


Scams and safety
The FTC counted $65 million in reported rental-scam losses in the year to mid-2025,
and about half of those scams started with a fake Facebook ad; another 16% started on
Craigslist. Which is awkward, because Facebook groups and Craigslist are also where
the real sublet and small-landlord inventory lives — the landing pad guide sends you
there on purpose. So don't avoid the channels; refuse to pay on them: never send money
for a unit you haven't physically walked through, never pay by wire, gift card, crypto, or
a payment app like Zelle to someone you haven't met, and treat "the landlord is out of
town" as the end of the conversation. Scammers now fake legitimacy with self-guided
lockbox tours of homes they don't control, so a tour alone proves nothing; check the
owner's name against the county assessor's record or the management company's own
site, and reverse-image-search the photos. Rent meaningfully below the table above is
bait, not luck.

At night, be careful around the Tenderloin, Civic Center, and SoMa near 6th Street;
Market around 6th is the roughest stretch. It's mostly open drug use and street disorder
rather than violence aimed at you, but walk a block over. Street View every exact
address before viewing, then walk the block at 9pm before signing. No listing site can do
that for you, and neither can this page.

Events: Luma is the rail
Jargon: Luma — the event platform (luma.com) that almost every SF tech event runs on.
RSVPs, waitlists, and host calendars all live there.

Most SF events never surface on Luma's public discover page — by some counts the
large majority are unlisted and only visible if you follow the host's calendar. So don't
browse; subscribe to 10–15 host calendars (see our Live Events Calendar page) and let
events come to you. Partiful is the second layer — house parties and socials rather than
talks — and note it genuinely gates on a US phone number, so sort your eSIM first (see
the apps page).

Filter hard. The test for a good event: a named host with a track record, a specific topic,
and an application or capped guest list. Red flags: "networking mixer" with no agenda,
open bar as the headline, 500+ RSVPs. The city runs a permanent circuit of ticketed
mega-mixers that fail every part of that test — they are theater, and I think the kindest
thing this guide can do is tell you to skip them entirely. One or two high-value events a
week beats five random ones. You are optimizing for repeat encounters with the same
people, not raw contact volume.
Many SF events now ask for your LinkedIn or a short application before approving you.
Fill these out properly — hosts curate the room, and a blank profile gets rejected. The
waitlists you'll see on half the good listings are the same mechanism: apply the day the
event posts, not the day before it happens.

The annual anchor is SF Tech Week (Oct 5–11, 2026, run by a16z). The public calendar
is maybe half of what's happening that week — the value is in unlisted side dinners, and
you get into those by asking the hosts of the small events you did attend.

The other half of the hidden graph is group chats — the WhatsApp and Discord threads
where hosts drop events before they're listed and intros get traded in real time. There is
no application form for these; you get added by someone who has seen you twice, which
is the repeat-encounters thesis converting into infrastructure. Once you have that
context, ask for the add explicitly — "is there a group where this crew coordinates?" is a
normal question here, not a presumptuous one.


Hackathons: the fastest way in for technical founders
SF runs weekend hackathons nearly every week — AI agents, voice, bio x AI, robotics.
Cerebral Valley and AI Tinkerers are the two rails to watch. A hackathon compresses six
months of networking into 48 hours: you build alongside people, and shared work
creates real trust in a way small talk never does. Demo something, even if it's rough —
the demo is the networking.


Coffee walks
The SF default for a first 1:1 is not a conference room; it's a 30–45 minute walk with
coffee. Suggest it explicitly: "Want to do a coffee walk near [neighborhood]?" It lowers
the stakes, and walking side-by-side makes candid conversation easier than sitting
across a table. On volume: Catarina Chia's 50-plus coffee chats over her first months
(field notes below) work out to three or four a week — I'd treat that as the ceiling, not
the target, if you're also running a company. Two or three, each ending with a concrete
next step or an intro you owe, compounds fine.

Cold DMs work in SF in a way they don't in most cities — but only done right. The
etiquette, per people who receive hundreds of them:

One founder's claim — that Twitter is the best way to tap into the tech SF network,

compounds with the public one: post a demo, a teardown, a real number, and the replies
start flowing toward you instead.


Dinners, and hosting before you feel ready
An 8-person curated dinner produces more real relationships than a 200-person mixer.
And there's a rung below hosting that costs nothing: volunteer. Most SF events run
understaffed — message the organizer on the Luma page and offer to work check-in or
take photos, and you've upgraded yourself from attendee to team, with a reason to talk
to everyone who walks in. Thank the organizers on your way out regardless; they're the
best-connected and least-thanked people in the room, and helpers get invited back,
sometimes onto the stage. Once you've met people you like, host one yourself — it's the
strongest networking move available to a newcomer, and you don't need to feel
established to do it. One hack from a serial dinner host: go around the table and have
each person introduce the person to their left, not themselves. Everyone has to have one
real conversation before dinner starts, and nobody gets to recite their pitch. Venues,
budgets, and the sponsor model are on the Where to Host Events page (21).


Recurring communities, and the rooms with doors
Relationships compound with frequency. A weekly community — same faces every
week — builds trust far faster than a monthly one. When choosing between two groups,
pick the one with weekly cadence. The desi founder communities (TiE SV, FUNDA,
SaaSBoomi US and the rest) are mapped with entry paths on the Desi Directory (16);
pick one anchor there, then make sure at least half your meetings by month three are
outside it — the reasoning is on the culture page (19).

Two physical rooms are worth knowing about specifically. Frontier Tower (995 Market
St) has become the gravitational center of the deep-tech scene: sixteen themed floors,
event venues on floors 2 and 16, and a calendar that runs multiple events a day, many
open to non-members via Luma. Membership — they call it citizenship — is
$190/month or $1,800/year, by application. You do not need to join to benefit; following
its open events calendar for a month is the cheap way to find out whether you'd use it.
SHACK15, upstairs in the Ferry Building, is the softer-edged founder club — coworking
with bay views by day, AI events and dinners by night. It's referral-gated: a member
nominates you, and membership last reported around $2,000/year. The honest path in
as a newcomer is a member's guest invite or one of its ticketed public events, not the
application form. Neither room is necessary; both are shortcuts to density if the free
channels are already working for you.
Sports and wellness
A large share of SF networking happens in workout clothes. Founders Running Club
meets Saturdays in the Panhandle — casual pace groups, walkers welcome, coffee after.
Run clubs, pickleball, South Bay cricket leagues (see the Weekends page), and cold
plunges all function as networking with the pitch pressure removed. Show up three
weeks in a row and you have a network.


If you're an operator, not a founder
Half this guide's readers are operators, and the channels reweight for you. "What are
you building?" still opens every conversation — have an answer about your craft or the
problem you own, because "I work at X" ends conversations here in a way it doesn't in
Bangalore. Recurring communities and coffee walks move up your list: hiring in this city
runs through the dinner-and-group-chat graph, and the person who pulls you into your
next role will be someone who has watched you think out loud three times, not a
recruiter. Hackathons still work if you're technical — shipping something on a weekend

If you live in the South Bay
Half this guide's housing and grocery pages assume you might land in Sunnyvale,
Fremont or Milpitas — cheaper, closer to the desi infrastructure, kinder to families. The
networking maths runs the other way. The founder scene has re-concentrated hard in SF
proper: on the Luma pull that opened this page, nearly every listing sat in the Mission,
SoMa, Yerba Buena or Dogpatch; Palo Alto and Stanford had one event each. What's local
to you is the thinner, older layer: TiE SV in Santa Clara, the cricket and run circuits on the
Weekends page, corporate-adjacent meetups. Catarina Chia's arc in the field notes below
is the honest template: she treated the Santa Clara commute as the price of the SF graph
and paid it deliberately. I'd recommend the same structure — one protected SF evening
a week, planned around the train (see Getting Around, 11), plus one local anchor with
weekly cadence — rather than pretending the two scenes are interchangeable. They
aren't.


The corridor twist: your evenings are India's mornings
SF events run 6–9pm, which is 6:30–9:30am IST the next day — exactly when your
Bangalore team wakes up and your standup wants you. This collision is where corridor
networking runs aground: people quietly stop showing up by month two. Solve it
structurally, not nightly: pick two protected event evenings a week and tell your India
team those are immovable, and move recurring India syncs to your early morning,
which is their evening. And spend your first-month advantage while you have it — "I
just moved to SF" is the best cold-outreach line you'll ever hold, and it expires (see the
Week 1 page).
The two rules that govern everything
Don't pitch first. The give-first norm is real here: lead with curiosity or a useful offer, and
let the other person ask what you're building. Pitching in the first five minutes marks
you as a taker. The full translation of this — and how to read the polite American no —
is on the culture page (19).

The 24-hour rule. Follow up within 24 hours of meeting someone, while they still
remember you. Reference something specific from the conversation and, ideally, include
something useful — a link, an intro offer. No generic "great to meet you." And past the
first dozen people, memory stops scaling: a plain spreadsheet of who, where, and what
you owe them beats every networking app.

The one-line version of both, from conor brennan-burke: "let the personal drive the
professional" — trust is the only scarce resource here, and it doesn't accrue to people
who treat every conversation as pipeline.

When to use what
Arriving at SFO: take BART — ~30 minutes to downtown for ~$11 vs $45–70 by Uber.

The car question
Living in SF city: don’t buy a car. Garage parking runs $300–500/month downtown,
street parking is a permit-and-sweeping-schedule nightmare, and SF has a notorious
smash-and-grab problem — thousands of car break-ins a month at peak (CBS News).
The rule everyone learns: never leave anything visible in a parked car, not even a
charging cable. Rent (Zipcar/Getaround/Turo) for weekend trips.
Living in Fremont/Sunnyvale/Santa Clara: you need a car. South Bay is built around
driving; transit exists but doubles or triples every trip. Get your California driver’s
license process started in month 1 — your Indian license works only temporarily.

Keep the Indian number alive
Every Indian bank, UPI app, mutual fund, DigiLocker and Aadhaar service authenticates
you with an SMS. Lose the number and you lose access to your Indian financial life, and
getting it back from California is a months-long problem routed through your bank's
overseas branch.

Two separate risks, and people conflate them.

The first is roaming. A SIM sitting in a drawer in India receives nothing. You need
international roaming active for the number to attach to a US network at all. Do it before
you fly — on some plans the activation itself triggers an OTP, and that's a loop you can't
break from a US IP address.

The second is deactivation, which is slower and takes the number permanently. Under
TRAI's rules a prepaid connection can't be cut for non-usage for 90 days as long as you
hold ₹20 or more. Past 90 days, ₹20 is deducted to buy another 30. Then 15 days of
grace. Then the number is deactivated and eventually recycled to someone else, and
every institution that authenticates you by SMS is authenticating them. Incoming calls
and SMS count as usage, so a founder receiving an OTP every few weeks is fine. One who
goes quiet for a quarter is not.

I'd convert to postpaid before flying if you're staying past six months. It costs more
monthly, removes the recycling risk entirely, and postpaid roaming packs carry 365-day

Tell your bank you're leaving
This costs the most to skip and appears on almost no checklist.

Under FEMA you cannot hold a resident savings account once your status changes to
NRI. It isn't triggered by a day count alone — leaving for business or an uncertain period
establishes intent, which for a founder means the day you land. Running a resident
account after that surfaces later as a frozen account or a KYC block, usually at the worst
moment.

In order:

   ●​ Redesignate your resident savings account as NRO. The account number usually
      survives; the card and cheque book get reissued.
   ●​ Open an NRE separately if you'll be sending USD home. You can't convert a
      resident account into an NRE, so do both in the same branch visit.
   ●​ Ask whether your bank will pre-stage the conversion against your visa. Some,
      ICICI among them, process NRO applications before departure for people with
      documented reason to move.
   ●​ Update your status with your AMC, demat and PAN records, or your folios freeze
      on the first post-move transaction.

Do this in a branch while you're still in India. The alternative is notarised or
consulate-attested forms, couriered originals, and at some banks a voice verification call
with a relationship manager. It works. It takes six weeks.


Move the money before you're an NRI
The LRS ceiling is USD 250,000 per PAN per financial year. That's not the constraint. TCS
is.

Tax Collected at Source is nil on your first ₹10 lakh of outward remittance in a financial
year, counted cumulatively across every bank, forex card and purpose you use. Above
₹10 lakh, anything that isn't education or medical is collected at 20% on the excess.

Do the maths before you wire. Move ₹40 lakh in one go and roughly ₹6 lakh is taken at
source. You get it back — TCS is an advance credit, it shows in your Form 26AS and
offsets your liability when you file — but "back" means after your return is processed,
which is a year of runway sitting with the tax department.

Three things that reduce it:

   ●​ The threshold resets on 1 April. Flying between January and March, split the
      transfer across the boundary and you get two ₹10 lakh windows.
   ●​ Quote your PAN on every transaction and check the PAN–Aadhaar link is live.
      Unlinked, the rate doubles.
   ●​ Spending abroad on an international credit card sits outside LRS, so it carries no
      TCS. That's a standing deferral rather than a repeal, so confirm it still holds
      before you build a plan around it.

I'm not a CPA and this isn't tax advice. If you're moving more than about ₹50 lakh, or
you'll be RNOR in your first year, the split-year residency question is worth an hour with
someone who does this professionally.


A US address that isn't your friend's couch
Mercury, Brex and Stripe all want a US mailing address before onboarding finishes, and
your first debit card needs somewhere to go. A virtual mailbox solves it for $10–30 a
month.

One real gate. USPS Form 1583 authorises a Commercial Mail Receiving Agency to
accept mail for you, and it needs notarising with two forms of ID. As a non-US applicant
you do this in an online notary session for $10–35, then the provider takes 1–5 business
days. Anytime Mailbox is the cheap marketplace option with 2,500+ locations of uneven
quality. Traveling Mailbox is a single operator, so scanning is consistent and included
rather than roughly $4 an item.

Be clear about what this address won't do. A CMRA address is widely rejected as proof
of residence by the DMV, by landlords, and by consumer banks opening a personal
account. It's a business mailing address and a package destination. You'll still need a
lease or utility bill for the DMV, and that's a week-three problem.


The IDP is a rental-counter document, not a licence
Get this one backwards and you'll drive unlicensed while feeling covered.

California doesn't require an International Driving Permit and recognises your Indian
licence outright while you're a non-resident. The IDP is a translation. It carries no
driving privilege of its own and extends no deadline.

What binds you is residency. Once you establish it — a lease, a job — Vehicle Code
§12505 gives you 10 days to hold a California licence. Not 10 days to start the
paperwork. And California has no licence reciprocity with India or anywhere else, so
you're taking the written test and the road test from scratch.

That leaves the IDP worth ₹1,000 for two reasons: rental agencies frequently ask for
one, and it's an English-language document a police officer can read. Get it if you'll rent a
car in month one. Skip it if you won't.

If you're getting one:
   ●​ ₹1,000 via Form 4A at the RTO with jurisdiction over the address on your driving
      licence. An address mismatch is the most common rejection, so file a change of
      address first if your DL still says your parents' house.
   ●​ Most states also want a Form 1-A medical certificate from a registered doctor,
      and many want your air ticket. You can't do this one very early.
   ●​ Parivahan handles the form and payment, but end-to-end online issuance isn't
      available in most states. Budget for an RTO visit and 3–7 working days.
   ●​ Valid one year from issue, or until your Indian licence expires, whichever comes
      first. Treat it as non-renewable from the US.


Health cover for the gap
You will land uninsured. Employer coverage doesn't exist if you're the employer, and
Covered California enrolment takes weeks even though moving to the US opens a 60-day
special enrolment window. The healthcare guide covers that part.

For the gap, buy a travel medical policy before you board. For a healthy person in their
thirties it runs roughly $1–5 a day. Take $100,000 minimum; US emergency care makes
lower limits meaningless.

Buy it in India. Policies bought after arrival typically impose a 5–10 day waiting period
on illness claims, which defeats the purpose. And be honest about what it is —
emergency and acute onset only, no routine care, no preventive care, pre-existing
conditions excluded. It's a four-to-eight week bridge. Running a year on travel medical is
how people end up with five-figure bills.


Entity, EIN and the bank account
Mercury opens accounts for non-US founders on a passport and an EIN. No SSN, no US
visit. It wants a real entity behind it, a live website, and a business description that reads
like an operating company — thin single-member LLCs with no web presence get held
or declined. If you are declined, replying with substance sometimes reopens it.
Resubmitting the same application unchanged does not.

The long pole is the EIN, not the bank. Without an SSN it's weeks. Start there and
Mercury takes days once it's in hand.

The structure decision — Delaware C-Corp versus LLC, flip versus fresh entity, ODI and
FEMA implications — is in the entity guide, and the banking and credit sequence is in
the money guide. Don't make that call off a checklist page.

get this from California? If no, it goes in.

Don't sign a lease from India. Photos lie, the block-to-block variation here is real, and
you haven't made the SF-versus-Peninsula call yet. Book a furnished month — co-living,
hacker house, monthly Airbnb — and decide from the ground. The landing pad guide
has the co-living maths and the neighbourhood decision tree.

I'd also flag something that tells you a lot. The consulate's own Contact Us page still lists
an old address on Arguello Boulevard while every other page on the same site says 71
Stevenson St. Nobody caught it. Before you drive anywhere, check twice.


If you lose your passport
Step one gates everything else, so do it right.

Get a police report, and get the correct kind. VFS wants a report that names you,
references the lost passport, carries the passport number, is approved rather than
pending, and is less than six months old. SFPD lets you file lost property online, but what
lands in your inbox is a temporary report that only becomes official once a reviewer
approves it. If you have any timeline at all, walk into a district station. A pending report
is not a report as far as VFS is concerned, and finding that out three weeks later is how
people miss weddings.

If you're not flying soon, apply for re-issue in lieu of lost, through VFS. Online form first,
then mail the physical file. The checklist is full of small trapdoors that will bounce it: the
envelope has to be superscribed "RE-ISSUANCE IN LIEU OF LOST PASSPORT", one
envelope holds exactly one application, prepaid FedEx labels aren't accepted, and you
need both an Indian and a US address on the form. Online registration before mailing is
mandatory.

On timing, the consulate's own position is that you shouldn't even enquire until 20
working days have gone by. That's four weeks of business days as the floor, not the
answer. A lost-passport re-issue also triggers police verification back in India, and that's
the step that wrecks every estimate. I'd plan for six to eight weeks. Give VFS your correct
Indian address with the right police station and district, because a sloppy address is the
most common reason verification stalls for months.

If you have to fly to India now, ask about an Emergency Certificate, but check first that
you qualify. The EC is a one-way travel document issued in a working day, same day in a
genuine emergency, as long as the consulate can confirm your passport particulars and
Indian nationality itself. If it can't, the file goes to India and the clock stops meaning
anything.

Here's what most write-ups get wrong. The EC is built primarily for people without valid
US status. Expired passport and no legal status. Deportation orders. A lost passport
where the holder has no valid visa and needs to get home. If you're on a live O-1 or H-1B
and you've lost your book, you are not the standard case, and the consular officer's
decision is final either way. Call the emergency line and ask before you plan around it.
And the obvious limit: an EC gets you to India. Coming back needs a new passport and a
new visa stamp.

Your visa stamp was inside that passport, and it's gone with it. That's a conversation
with an immigration attorney, not a webpage.

Scan your passport, visa and I-94 into cloud storage. Print one set and leave it
somewhere that isn't your bag. It turns a crisis into paperwork, which is the best
outcome available.

path to fatal — ER. Fever, a sprain, an infection, stitches, a UTI — urgent care, walk in, no
appointment. If you're stable and it can wait two hours, urgent care.

The ambulance is a separate bill from a separate company. That surprises almost
everyone the first time.

Uninsured patients get charged at list price, roughly two to two and a half times what an
insurer pays for identical care. Which is where the following four things become useful,
and none of them are widely known.

Under AB 716, in effect since January 2024, a ground ambulance cannot bill an
uninsured patient more than the Medi-Cal or Medicare rate, whichever is greater.
Insured on a California-regulated plan, you owe only your in-network cost share even
when the ambulance is out of network. The gap is self-funded employer plans, which are
federally regulated and sit outside this.

Under the Hospital Fair Pricing Act, uninsured patients at or below 400% of the federal
poverty line get charity care or discounted payment, and can't be charged more than the
hospital would have received from Medi-Cal. Be honest about whether that's you. A
funded founder on a Bay salary isn't. But the same law has a second door labelled "high
medical costs" — out-of-pocket at that hospital exceeding 10% of your family income.
One bad ER visit in year one, before you're paying yourself properly, clears that bar
more easily than you'd think.

A hospital can't report your debt to a credit agency or sue you for 180 days from the first
bill. For ambulance debt it's 150. When you're building US credit from zero, that window
is the whole difference between an annoying month and a two-year problem.

And under EMTALA, any hospital that takes Medicare has to stabilise you in an
emergency regardless of insurance or status. A hospital bill is private debt. Don't let
arithmetic keep you out of an emergency room — that is the one place on this page
where doing the cautious financial thing is the wrong call.

Ask for an itemised bill and the self-pay discount before you pay anything. Never pay the
first number you're shown.

Find your nearest urgent care on Maps this week, while nothing is wrong. And get
insured — the 60-day enrolment clock is in the Day 0–7 checklist.


Break-ins, landlords, and crashes
Locked out, no heat, burst pipe: your landlord or the building's emergency line first,
which is why you save it the day you sign the lease. SF tenants can escalate habitability
complaints to 311.

Car break-in: photograph everything before you touch anything, then file. SFPD takes
vehicle burglary, theft, vandalism and vehicle tampering online. Stolen vehicles, or
anything with a known suspect, need a call or a station visit. Fremont, San Jose and most
Peninsula cities run their own online systems. Then the rule every long-timer repeats
and every newcomer ignores exactly once: nothing visible in a parked car. Not a bag, not
a jacket, not a charging cable.

Crash: get to safety, 911 if anyone is hurt, photos and insurance details. Don't discuss
fault at the scene with anyone.


The earthquake setup
You'll feel one. Almost all of them are nothing. The setup takes four minutes and you do
it once.

MyShake sends a ShakeAlert warning for magnitude 4.5 and up, a few seconds ahead of
the shaking. Free, both platforms, built at UC Berkeley. Android already has ShakeAlert
in the OS under Settings, then Safety and emergency, then Earthquake alerts. Turn it on
either way. Drop, cover, hold on. You don't run outside.

Then sign up for your county's alerts. In San Francisco, text your ZIP to 888-777 or use
alertsf.org. Alameda runs AC Alert, San Mateo has SMC Alert, Santa Clara has AlertSCC.
Register more than one ZIP — where you sleep and where you work.

19. SF Culture for Indian Founders
SF culture for Indian founders and
operators
Stage: Ongoing · Type: Guide · v2, July 2026 · Verified July 2026

The unwritten rules of professional life in the Bay, translated for someone arriving from India.

Two founders land at SFO the same week. One has $2M ARR, 100 paying customers, 40%
margins. The other finished at Stanford, did three years at Stripe, has no product. Six
months later the second is closing a Series A and the first still can't get meetings.

That's Thiyagarajan Maruthavan's example, not mine. He runs Upekkha out of SF and
Bangalore and says he watches it repeat every month. His explanation: the Valley is "a
network that occasionally manifests as a market."

The optimistic version is also true. NFAP counted 96 US unicorns with Indian-origin founders
in June 2026, more than any other country of origin. But roughly a quarter of all US
billion-dollar companies have a founder who first arrived as an international student, and
most of that Indian list landed at 22, spent a decade inside the network, then started
something. Arriving at 34 with a company that already works is the harder version of the
same move. Culture is the part of the gap you can close in months rather than years.


The mismatch everything else comes from
Erin Meyer's culture map puts the US at the far explicit end. Americans spell out the obvious,
put it in writing, say it twice. The same map puts them on the indirect side for negative
feedback.

That pairing is what costs people months. The most explicit culture in the world about
information is one of the most cushioned about verdicts. Nobody will tell you no. They will tell
you everything else, at length, and you will walk out believing something happened.


Your first conversations
Leading with the pitch signals seriousness in Bangalore. Here it signals that you've classified
the person as a lead. The sequence is relationship, then trust, then ask. First conversations
are for curiosity, and if they're interested they will ask what you're building.

Have the 15-second answer ready before you land. Plain English, no category jargon, ends
with a question back. "What are you building?" is the standard opener in this city and you'll
answer it several times a week.

Every meeting opens with two or three minutes of small talk. Skipping it to get to business
reads as transactional. It's the handshake, not the delay.
The give-first norm is the load-bearing one. Steve Blank traces it to Bob Noyce taking calls
from a 20-year-old Steve Jobs: people here help strangers and expect the ecosystem, rather
than the individual, to repay it. You arrive as a beneficiary of that. You become a member of
it by giving before you need anything — an intro, a real answer in a group chat, a document
you already have sitting in Drive.

Send the calendar invite within the hour. "Let's get coffee" is vapor until an invite exists with
a location or a link on it. Rescheduling with notice is fine. Silent no-shows get remembered.

Smaller things that mark you as new: physical business cards now read slightly badly at tech
events, and the swap is LinkedIn or a phone contact. Dress smart casual with a layer you
can shed, since the temperature moves 20–30°F between the South Bay and SF in an
hour's drive. And get the geography right — Silicon Valley is the South Bay, the Peninsula
runs roughly Palo Alto to Daly City, SF is neither.

Where this breaks: at demo days, office hours and accelerator sessions, pitching
immediately is the protocol, and holding back reads as unprepared. The no-pitch rule is for
unstructured rooms, which is most of them.


The polite no
Investors here almost never say no to your face. There's no upside in it — the market is
small, founders talk, and the company they pass on today might be one their best portfolio
founder drags them into in eighteen months. The door stays technically open and the
decision is closed.

Bills split evenly by default. Nobody is embarrassed by it, you aren't expected to host, and
insisting on paying for the table can land as a status move rather than as hospitality. If
someone says they've got it, accept and get the next one.

On tipping, use the real number. Toast's Q1 2026 transaction data puts the average at 19.3%
at full-service restaurants and 15.8% at quick service. Twenty percent at a sit-down meal
never reads badly; counter service, the suggested button or round up.
The trap is the payment rail. Venmo is the social default, and Venmo cannot complete
identity verification without an SSN or ITIN. You can still send and receive using a linked
bank account or card, but Venmo may demand an SSN once you're sending $300 or more in
a rolling seven-day window, which one founder dinner and two group Ubers will clear. Install
it in week one, expect the cap until your SSN or ITIN lands, and run the arithmetic through
Splitwise meanwhile. Sequencing is on the money and ITIN page.


Hierarchy is genuinely flat
Interns email partners. New arrivals DM famous founders. Titles buy nothing in conversation,
first names go for everyone including people twenty years senior, and visible deference
reads as weakness rather than courtesy. You don't wait to be invited before approaching
someone — the approach is the norm.

The inverse gets forgotten. Be as generous with the 22-year-old nobody as with the partner,
because in this town they're frequently next year's somebody and people remember which
version of you they met.


The part nobody writes down
Pattern matching is documented rather than imagined. A UCLA Anderson study of more
than 93,000 potential VC-founder deals found that facial similarity between investor and
founder lifted the odds of getting funded by around 10%, holding shared age, ethnicity and
gender constant. The same analysis found that a 10-point rise in the share of investors
closely resembling a founder came with a 14% drop in the odds of a successful exit. That
second number is the one to hold onto. It's a defect in their process rather than a verdict on
you.

Your credentials also don't travel. IIT and IIM are known quantities to Indian investors and to
Indian-origin operators here; to a generalist partner they're a line on a page. Maruthavan's
framing is that roots come from an education layer, an experience layer or an ambition layer,
and with none of the three locally you start below zero rather than at it. Joining things fixes it,
over roughly a year.

One call of my own: the diaspora is the fastest way to land and the slowest way to build a
US customer base, if it's the only network you build. It's easy to spend a year inside desi
founder WhatsApp groups feeling well connected while having met nobody who could be a
customer or a co-founder. If fewer than half your meetings by month three are outside the
diaspora, correct it deliberately.

   ●​ Read the polite-no table before your first investor meeting, and again after it
   ●​ Make "calendar invite within the hour" your permanent default
   ●​ Install Venmo now, set 20% as your mental default, keep Splitwise for group maths
      until your SSN lands
   ●​ Ask one person this week what the strongest reason your thing fails is, then sit

   ●​ At the end of month three, count what share of your meetings were outside the

Nobody tells you
   ●​ The only real yes from an investor is speed. A concrete next step within days.
      Everything warm and unscheduled is a no.
   ●​ Who introduced you matters more than people admit. The Bay is tribal, and a
      sponsor beats any amount of cold outreach. Give-first isn't charity, it's how sponsors
      get acquired.
   ●​ Being liked is not the same as being funded or hired. You can have a wonderful year

      has no natural stopping point built into it, so you have to build one.

21. Where to Host Events
Stage: Ongoing · Type: Guide · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: How to get a venue in SF for your own event — from free VC
offices to paid private rooms — plus a checklist for running your first 30-person
gathering.
Hosting is the networking cheat code: the host meets everyone, controls the guest list,
and earns give-first credit with the whole room. In SF, venues are more gettable than
newcomers assume — much of the event economy runs on borrowed and sponsored
space.

Venue types, cheapest first
1. VC and startup offices (free)
Many VC firms and well-funded startups lend their office/event space to community
events — it’s deal flow and branding for them. The ask is a short, specific email:
     Subject: Venue ask — [community] founder meetup, [date range] We’re
     hosting a 30-person meetup of [specific audience — e.g., India-origin AI
     founders] on a weekday evening in [month]. We handle Luma page, check-in,
     and cleanup; we’d credit [firm] as host and you’re welcome to say a 2-minute
     hello. Would your event space be available?
What they get: audience access and association. What you promise: a real audience,
their logo on the Luma page, and leaving the space clean. Start with firms where any
member has a relationship — warm beats cold here as everywhere.

2. Coworking and community spaces (free to cheap, sometimes paid)
•​ Frontier Tower (995 Market St) — 16-floor deep-tech hub with dedicated event
     venues and 3+ events a day; community-aligned events are its whole model. Ask
     about the Spaceship (floor 2) and Lounge (floor 16).
•​ SHACK15 (Ferry Building) — member club with a strong events program; you’ll
     need a member connection to host, which makes it a good “invite via TFF” ask.
•​ Generic coworking chains rent event space by the hour; fine as a fallback but you
     pay list price. [Specific rate cards: needs verification.]

3. Restaurants and private dining rooms (for dinners)
Private dining rooms (PDRs) typically work on a minimum spend — you commit to a
food-and-drink total rather than a room fee. SF benchmarks from venue marketplaces:
roughly $500–$5,000 minimum depending on room and night, averaging around
$3,000; examples include ~$1,500 minimums with ~$125/head family-style menus at
mid-range spots, up to $3,500 minimums at name restaurants. Rules of thumb:

Dinner size                                   Realistic budget
8–10 people                                   $800–1,500 (often no PDR needed —
                                              book a big table)
12–20 people                                $1,500–3,500 minimum spend
20–40 people                                $3,000+ or partial buyout

Tuesday–Thursday nights get you better minimums than weekends. Ask for the events
manager, not the host stand.
Jargon: Buyout — renting the entire restaurant for a flat fee or high minimum. Minimum
spend — the amount you must spend on food/drink to hold the room; you pay the
difference if the group under-orders.

4. Parks and outdoor (nearly free)
For picnics, walks, and casual gatherings, SF Rec & Parks rules: under 25 people with
no rented equipment, you generally don’t need a permit. Over 25 (or bringing
equipment), reserve a picnic site — small-group sites start around $25, bookable up to
90 days out; 100+ attendees needs a large-picnic application. A reserved Golden Gate
Park picnic site is the cheapest 30-person venue in the city.

5. The sponsor model (someone else pays)
The standard SF pattern: a vendor (Brex, cloud providers, dev-tool companies, law
firms, banks) pays for venue and food in exchange for audience access — a logo on
the Luma page, a 2-minute intro, a table. Brex’s Supper Club series is this model
productized: they fund founder dinners in 15+ cities because founders are their
customers. To get sponsored: write a one-pager with your audience (who, how many,
seniority), the format, and what the sponsor gets. Startup-facing vendors have budget
for exactly this; the bar is a credible, specific audience.

Your first 30-person event: the checklist
•​   T-3 weeks: lock date and venue (weekday evening, 6:30–8:30pm), publish the

•​   RSVPs: cap at ~2x room capacity — SF flake rates mean 60 RSVPs yields roughly

•​   Day of: name tags (with "ask me about ___"), a check-in list, water/snacks, one
     co-host working the door while you work the room.
•​   T+1 day: follow-up post — thank-you, photo, one takeaway, next event date. This is
     what turns an event into a series.

Medicines: the rules first

•​   Declare all medication to CBP (Customs and Border Protection) on arrival.

•​   Carry prescription copies and a doctor’s letter (English) for anything beyond
     basic OTC — name, condition, dosage.
•​   ~90-day supply is the practical ceiling for personal-use medication; controlled
     substances have a hard cap of 50 dosage units.
•​   A medicine being legal in India does not make it importable — but personal-use
     quantities with documentation are routinely allowed.

Packing: clothes for a city with five climates

Schools: your address is the admission letter

25. Taxes Across the Corridor
25. Taxes Across the Corridor
Stage: Operating · Type: Guide · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: Orientation, not advice. The year you move, you’re a tax resident
of somewhere in both countries — this page maps who taxes what, which forms exist,
and when you need professionals on each side.

The one-sentence version
The year you move, India taxes you on Indian income, the US starts taxing you on
worldwide income once you become a US tax resident, and the DTAA plus foreign tax
credits stop you from paying twice on the same rupee — if you file correctly on both
sides.
Jargon: The DTAA (Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement) is the 1989 India–US treaty
that assigns taxing rights and lets each country credit tax paid to the other.

When the US starts counting you
Two ways to become a US tax resident: 1. Green card test — you hold one. (Not you,
yet.) 2. Substantial Presence Test (SPT) — a day-count: you’re a resident if you spend
≥31 days in the US this year AND (days this year) + ⅓(days last year) + ⅙(days year
before) ≥ 183.
Practical translation: land on an O-1 in, say, August and stay, and you’ll typically cross
into residency during your first or second calendar year. First-year movers often file a
dual-status return (part-year non-resident, part-year resident) — this is exactly the
return you should not DIY.
Once resident, the US taxes your worldwide income: Indian rent, Indian mutual fund
gains, Indian interest — all reportable in the US.

Your Indian status: NRI, and the RNOR trapdoor (for later)
India counts days too: spend less than 182 days in India in a financial year (with some
conditions) and you’re an NRI (Non-Resident Indian) — India then taxes only your
India-sourced income, not your US salary.
Jargon: RNOR (Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident) is India’s transition status — you
count as an Indian resident by day-count, but your foreign income stays untaxed in
India.
RNOR matters at two moments: - The year you leave: if you depart late in India’s
April–March financial year, you may still be an Indian resident that year. Timing your
move before ~September 28 (keeping India days under 182) usually lands you NRI
status for the departure year — worth planning with your CA. - If you ever move back:
returning NRIs typically get RNOR status for up to ~3 financial years, during which
foreign income (US capital gains, RSU sales) stays out of India’s net — the single biggest
tax-planning window in the corridor. File it away.

Visa denial or RFE: it’s not over
Jargon: RFE (Request for Evidence) — USCIS asking for more proof before deciding your
petition. It is not a denial; most RFEs that get thorough responses still end in approval.
If you get an RFE: you typically get up to 87 days to respond (needs verification per
notice — the deadline is on your notice). Respond once, completely, with your attorney.
Do not treat it as a rejection signal to investors — RFEs on O-1 and H-1B petitions are
routine.
If you get a denial, three paths:

A denial is not a ban. Nothing stops a stronger refiling, and many founders get approved
on attempt two with better evidence. The prior filing stays in your record, so the new
petition must address why the last one failed — don’t just resubmit the same packet. If
you were in the US when denied, your status question is separate and urgent: talk to
your attorney the same day about lawful presence.

Runway running out: the honest sequence
At 6 months of runway, not 2:
1.​ Tell your investors the truth early. The conversation founders dread — “we have
    6 months and plan B/C/D” — is one investors have monthly. Early honesty is what
    makes bridge money possible; surprise is what kills it.
2.​ Bridge options, roughly in order: inside round / bridge note from existing
    investors, revenue push + deep cost cut to extend 6→12 months, acqui-hire
    conversations (start these while you still have leverage), or a structured
    wind-down.
3.​ Founders on visas have a shorter fuse than the company does. Your personal
    runway math must include the visa: if the company dies, your clock starts (below).
    Cut burn earlier than a US-citizen founder would.

Shutting down properly
If it comes to it, how you shut down is remembered longer than why. The mechanics for
a Delaware C-corp:
1.​ Board resolution + stockholder consent approving dissolution and a plan of
    dissolution.
2.​ Pay all Delaware franchise taxes and file all annual reports — Delaware won’t
    accept your Certificate of Dissolution until these are current. File before
    December 31: slipping one day into January triggers a full extra year of franchise
    tax and filings.
3.​ File the Certificate of Dissolution (short form only if no stock issued and no debts
    — rare for a funded startup; you’ll likely file long form).
4.​ Wind up: pay creditors, terminate contracts and payroll, withdraw foreign
    qualifications (e.g., California), distribute remaining assets per the waterfall.
5.​ Final tax returns: federal (marked final), Delaware, California; issue final
    W-2s/1099s. Services like SimpleClosure and Carta’s dissolution guides exist
    because founders reliably miss steps.
Budget 2–4 months and real money for a clean shutdown. Investors respect a clean one;
a zombie C-corp accruing franchise tax follows you into your next company.

Your visa when the company dies
The company’s death starts your personal countdown:
•​   H-1B, O-1, L-1, E-2 and similar: a discretionary grace period of up to 60
     consecutive days (or until your I-94 expires, whichever is sooner) after
     employment ends. In it you can: find a new sponsor (H-1B transfer lets you start on
     filing; O-1 does not — you wait for approval), file a change of status (B-2 as a
     bridge is common), or leave.
•​   The grace period is discretionary, not guaranteed — USCIS can shorten it. Don’t
     run it to day 59.
•​   If you’re the founder on an O-1 sponsored by your own dying company, the
     petitioner is disappearing — get attorney advice before dissolution filing, and
     sequence the company’s death after your own status plan is in motion.

Going back to India with dignity
Going back is a move, not a failure. The Bay Area is full of respected operators who did a
US stint, returned, and built from Bengaluru — the returned-founder network in India is
now deep, funded, and hiring.
Practical reverse logistics:
•​   Banking: your NRE/NRO accounts convert back — NRE becomes resident (or RFC
     — Resident Foreign Currency — account for parking dollars); tell your bank on
     return. US accounts can stay open; keep one US bank account and a US phone
     number for credit history and payouts.
•​   Tax: you may be RNOR (Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident) for ~2–3 years back
     in India — foreign income largely stays out of Indian tax during that window
     (needs verification for your dates). File a final US tax return; consider US exit items
     (401k stays and grows; don’t cash it out at 30% penalty+tax without advice).
•​   The story: “I built a company in SF for 3 years” is a credential in Bengaluru, not a
     scar. Write the honest postmortem — the founders who process the failure openly
     (see Hudson’s essay below) recover faster and raise again. Don’t do a rebound
     startup in month one.

27. First 10 US Customers
27. First 10 US Customers — GTM for India→US Founders
Stage: Operating · Type: Guide · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: The question every corridor founder asks TFF: “How do I get my
first 10 US customers?” This is the tactical answer — what to unlearn from India, and
what to do instead.
Jargon: GTM (go-to-market) = your plan for finding, convincing, and closing customers.
“Corridor founder” = a founder running an India→US company.

Unlearn your India GTM instincts
Three instincts that worked in India will hurt you here:
1. Relationship-selling vs process-selling. In India, deals close over long
relationships, chai, and a champion who “knows the MD.” US mid-market buyers run a
process: discovery call → demo → security review → procurement. Your relationship
gets you the first meeting; the process closes the deal. Learn the process.
2. Discounting culture vs value pricing. Indian buyers negotiate everything;
discounting is expected. US buyers read a heavy discount as a signal the product is weak.
A useful benchmark from cross-border pricing analysis: a dollar of willingness-to-pay in
India equates to roughly $7 in the US market. Price in USD, confidently, at US
benchmarks — not your India price converted. If your India price was ₹50k/yr, your
US price is probably $5k–$15k/yr, not $600.
3. “Free pilot” reflex. US buyers distrust free. Cheap and free both signal “not serious.”
Charge something from day one (see design partners below).

Founder-led sales: the first 10 are yours
Nobody should sell your first 10 deals but you. Data across founder surveys is
consistent: the #1 source of first customers is warm intros (friends, ex-colleagues,
investors); cold outbound is #2 — and most successful startups stayed founder-led until
~$1M ARR.
Work warm first. List every US person you know: batchmates now at Google/Stripe,
your investors’ portfolio founders, IIT/NIT/BITS alumni Slacks, SaaSBoomi network.
Ask for specific intros (“Do you know a Head of RevOps at a 50–500 person company?”),
not “anyone who might be interested.”

Run discovery like a professional
Jargon: Discovery call = the first sales call, where you diagnose the buyer’s problem
before showing product.
Structure: 5 min rapport → 20 min questions about their current workflow, pain, and
cost of the problem → 5 min on whether a next step makes sense. Listen 70%, talk
30%. Do not demo on the first call unless asked — a demo before diagnosis is a
feature tour, not a sale.

Design partners: charge something
Jargon: Design partner = an early customer who shapes the product in exchange for
influence and a discount.
The trap: unpaid “design partners” who give feedback forever and never convert. The
fix, per Bessemer and Common Paper guidance: cap the cohort (5–10), time-box it
(8–12 weeks), and charge upfront — a 10–50% discount off intended pricing, never
free. Upfront payment is the strongest signal the pain is real.

Use the SF advantage
You moved here for a reason: your buyer’s champion is at an event this week. Check
Luma/Partiful for your category’s meetups; go where your buyers (not other founders)
hang out. A 10-minute in-person conversation replaces five cold emails. Ask every
customer call to end with “who else should I talk to?” — US buyers give referrals freely
when the product delivers.

Timezone strategy if your team is in India
•​   Founder takes all US-hours calls — 8am–6pm PT is sacred selling time.
•​   India team’s day ends as yours begins: bugs reported in a US demo at 2pm PT are
     fixed overnight IST and shipped before the next demo. Sell this as a feature (“we fix
     issues overnight”).

The middle options: contractors and EOR

EOR (Employer of Record). A provider — Deel, Rippling, Remote — legally employs

compliance calendars, two benefit systems. US payroll via Gusto/Rippling; India
payroll via your subsidiary (or EOR). Budget for a US accountant and an Indian CA who

31. SSN, ITIN & Driver’s License Walkthroughs
Stage: Month 1 · Type: Quick-reference · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: Three bureaucratic errands, step by step, in the order to run
them. Total elapsed time if you start day one: SSN in ~3–4 weeks, license in ~4–8 weeks,
spouse’s ITIN at first tax filing.

Walkthrough A: SSN (Social Security Number)
Who’s eligible: anyone with US work authorization — O-1, H-1B, L-1, E-2, F-1 with
valid work authorization. Not eligible: O-3/H-4 spouses without work authorization
(they use an ITIN instead; H-4 with EAD does qualify).
1.​ Wait ~10 days after landing. SSA verifies your status against DHS entry records,
    which take days to sync. Apply too early and you get denied and sent home for a
    second visit.
2.​ Start the application online at ssa.gov (you can begin it before your office visit),
    then visit an SSA office — several in SF; check hours, go early.
3.​ Bring: passport, printed I-94 (download from the CBP website after every entry),
    and your visa approval notice (I-797 for O-1/H-1B). Originals, not copies.
4.​ The visit itself is ~30 minutes. You’ll get a receipt letter with your number on it in
    some cases; the card arrives by mail in ~2–4 weeks.
5.​ When it arrives: add the SSN to your bank accounts, credit cards, payroll, and —
    see Walkthrough C — the DMV record.
You can start work before the card arrives; the number’s issuance, not the physical card,
is what matters to payroll.

Walkthrough B: ITIN (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number)
Who needs it: people with US tax obligations but no work authorization — typically
your O-3/dependent spouse (needed to file a joint return, which usually saves real
money), or occasionally a founder abroad with US-source income.
1.​ You generally can’t apply on a whim — the W-7 form must accompany a federal
    tax return, unless you qualify for an exception (e.g., certain treaty or bank-reporting
    cases).
2.​ Standard route: file your first US tax return (April after your first year) with your
    spouse’s W-7 attached, marking the return for ITIN processing.
3.​ Better route — use a CAA. Jargon: A Certified Acceptance Agent is an
    IRS-authorized person who verifies passports in person, so you don’t mail the
    original passport to the IRS. Find one via the IRS acceptance-agent list; many
    corridor CPA firms are CAAs.
4.​ Timeline: the IRS takes roughly 7–11 weeks to issue the ITIN, up to ~14 weeks in
    the January–April crush. Apply with an early-filed return, not on April 14.
5.​ After issuance: the ITIN unlocks joint filing, bank accounts, and ITIN-friendly
    credit products for your spouse (see article 6).
The mail-your-passport option technically exists. Don’t — your spouse loses their
passport for 2+ months.

Walkthrough C: California driver’s license
California expects new residents to get a CA license within 10 days of establishing
residency — loosely enforced, but insurance and police stops make a local license
genuinely useful fast. Your Indian license plus an IDP is a short-term bridge only; CA
does not exchange foreign licenses — everyone takes both tests.
1.​ Book a DMV appointment at dmv.ca.gov the week you land — average waits run
    ~2 weeks statewide, but busy Bay Area offices can run 30+ days. You can also walk
    in; expect a long morning.
2.​ Do the online pre-application (saves an hour at the office) and study the CA
    Driver’s Handbook — the knowledge test has real fail rates for people who wing it.
3.​ First visit — documents: passport + visa, I-94, proof of SSN (or SSA ineligibility
    letter for spouses), and two proofs of CA residency (lease, utility bill, bank
    statement with your address). Apply for the REAL ID version — visa holders are
    eligible, and it’s the license that boards domestic flights after the REAL ID
    enforcement regime. Jargon: REAL ID is the federally accepted version of a state
    license, marked with a bear-and-star.
4.​ Pass the knowledge (written) test — done at a testing station the same visit;
    recent format is ~36 questions with ~83% to pass (format changes; needs
    verification). You then get an interim permit.
5.​ Book the behind-the-wheel test — separate appointment, often 2–6 weeks out.
    You must arrive in an insured, registered car with a licensed driver. Rental cars are
    usually not accepted; use a friend’s insured car or a driving-school car.
6.​ Pass, and the license arrives by mail in a few weeks. Visa holders get a “Limited
    Term” license that expires with your authorized stay — extend your visa, then
    renew the license.
Common fail point: Indians with 10+ years of driving fail the behind-the-wheel test on
American ritual, not skill — full stops behind the limit line, exaggerated head-turn
shoulder checks, yielding to pedestrians early. Take one hour with a local driving
instructor before the test; they know the examiner script.

•​   ☐ Day 1: download your I-94 from the CBP site and save it — every walkthrough

•​   ☐ Day 1: book the DMV appointment (longest queue) and start the SSA online
     application
•​   ☐ Day ~10: SSA office visit with passport, I-94, I-797
•​   ☐ Week 2–3: DMV first visit — pre-application done, two residency proofs, take

•​   ☐ At tax time: file spouse’s W-7 through a CAA with your joint return
Nobody tells you
•​   The SSA “ineligibility letter” matters: your non-working spouse should visit SSA
     anyway to get a formal denial letter — the DMV accepts it in place of an SSN for
     their license, and some banks want it too.
•​   Sequence the DMV around your SSN: the DMV electronically verifies SSNs, and a

You’re an NRI now — your Indian accounts are technically illegal
Jargon: NRI (Non-Resident Indian) — under FEMA (India’s Foreign Exchange
Management Act), you become one when you leave India for an uncertain period or stay
abroad 182+ days in a financial year. Moving to the US as a founder qualifies from
roughly day 1 — intent matters, not just day count.
FEMA does not allow an NRI to hold a regular resident savings account. You must
convert or restructure:

Mechanics: your existing savings account can only be converted to NRO — for an NRE
account you open a fresh one. Most banks (ICICI, HDFC, SBI, Axis) accept conversion
requests with visa copy, passport, and overseas address proof; some allow it fully online
(needs verification per bank).
The penalty for ignoring this: FEMA Section 13 allows penalties up to 3x the amount
involved (or ₹2 lakh where unquantifiable) plus ₹5,000/day for continuing violations.
Enforcement against small accounts is rare, but banks increasingly freeze accounts
flagged as non-resident, and the paper trail matters when you later repatriate large
sums or sell property.

Supporting parents: the mechanics
•​   Direct Wise/Remitly transfers to their resident account — simplest; money sent
     to parents is a gift and gifts from a child (a “relative”) are tax-free for them in
     India, any amount.
•​   Or fund your NRO account and set standing instructions to their account.
•​   US side: gifts above the US annual exclusion (~$19,000 per recipient in 2026 —
     needs verification) require you to file a gift-tax form (Form 709); rarely any tax
     owed, but the filing matters.
•​   Set a monthly auto-transfer and stop treating it as a decision each month.

India → US: the LRS
Jargon: LRS (Liberalised Remittance Scheme) — RBI’s rule letting each Indian resident
send up to $250,000 per financial year abroad. Relevant when your parents fund you,
or you move your own resident money before conversion. TCS (tax collected at source)
applies above ₹10 lakh/year — 20% for general remittances as of April 2026 (claimable
back in their ITR). Your parents gifting you money for the startup runs through LRS —
plan the TCS cash-flow hit.

Nobody tells you
•​   Interest earned on an NRE account is tax-free in India — but fully taxable in the
     US. Your “tax-free” NRE FD is not tax-free once you’re a US tax resident, and you
     must report Indian accounts on FBAR/FATCA forms if balances cross $10k
     aggregate.
•​   The single most expensive mistake isn’t fees — it’s keeping money in Indian mutual
     funds as a US tax resident. PFIC treatment can tax gains at top rates plus interest
     charges. Many NRIs quietly exit MFs before their first US tax year ends.
•​   Remitly’s headline “zero fee” isn’t free — the margin is in the exchange rate. Always
     compare the final ₹ delivered, not the fee line.

Jet-lag protocol for founders who work on landing
India is +12.5 hours from California — the worst possible shift. The direction rules:
Flying SFO→India (eastward-ish, advance your clock): - 3 days before: shift sleep
30–60 min earlier each night. - On landing: morning outdoor light, 20–30 min before
10am local. Avoid bright light after 8pm. - Eat on local meal times from meal 1, even if
not hungry — food entrains the body clock. - Melatonin 0.5 mg at local bedtime for 3–5
nights (low dose works as well as 5 mg).
Flying India→SFO (westward, delay your clock): - Easier direction. On landing: get
evening light — walk after dinner, sit near windows late. - Stay up until at least 9:30pm
local no matter what. The 6pm crash is the enemy; caffeine cutoff 2pm. - First morning:
outdoor light can wait until mid-morning; don’t blast yourself at 6am.
Working rule: schedule no important calls in the first 36 hours either direction. Day 2
afternoon is when your brain returns.

Carry-on checklist — SFO → India
•​   Passport + valid US visa stamp (see re-entry note below), OCI/Indian passport for
     spouse/kids as applicable
•​   If your visa stamp is expired: your renewal appointment confirmation and full
     petition paperwork
•​   Prescription meds in original packaging + doctor’s letter
•​   Gifts: stay under India’s duty-free limit — ₹75,000 (~$875) of goods under
     Baggage Rules 2026 (effective Feb 2, 2026), plus one new laptop duty-free (a
     second laptop counts against the ₹75,000)
•​   India customs one-liner: gold jewellery allowance is 40g (women) / 20g (men) by
     weight, jewellery only — bars and coins get taxed; excess attracts a flat ~10% duty
     under the 2026 rules

Carry-on checklist — India → SFO
•​   Passport with unexpired visa stamp matching your current status — an
     approval notice (I-797) alone doesn’t board you; the stamp does. If your stamp
     expired, you needed a consulate appointment this trip.
•​   I-797 approval notice original, employment/company evidence, recent pay stubs —
     CBP can ask
•​   3–6 month med supply with prescriptions; no more than 90-day supply per drug is
     the safe rule for US entry (needs verification)
•​   Food: packaged/sealed only, declare everything agricultural — declaring is free, not
     declaring is a fine
•​   Homemade ghee/pickles/sweets: sealed and declared usually fine; meat and fresh
     produce never

34. Wellness & Isolation — Done Properly
Stage: Ongoing · Type: Guide · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: The honest version of what months 3–12 feel like, and the
specific structures — therapy, peer groups, routines — that keep founders functional.
Tactical, not preachy.

What actually happens
Three things compound in your first year, and none of them show up in the relocation
checklists:
1.​ Everyone here is in a bubble too. The Bay Area is full of transplants running the
    same experiment. Friendships form slower than in India — people are friendly,
    calendars are full, nothing sticks without repetition. Realistic timeline to a real local
    network: 6+ months of consistent effort.
2.​ Founder isolation is structural, not personal. Roughly 1 in 3 startup CEOs report
    having no one to talk to about the hardest parts of the job; over half of founders
    report anxiety. You can’t tell employees (they panic), investors (they price it in), or
    your parents (they worry). That’s the design flaw; fix it with structure.
3.​ Distance hits on a schedule. Diwali, Holi, Rakhi, weddings, a parent’s health scare
    at 3am India time. The homesickness isn’t constant — it spikes on festival days and
    family events. Plan for the spikes.

Therapy: the cost reality

Founder peer structures
One-off networking events don’t fix isolation; recurring rooms do.
•​   A peer group of 4–8 founders meeting monthly beats 50 coffee chats. Options:
     YC/accelerator batch groups, Entrepreneurs’ Organization, curated masterminds,
     or self-assemble from your own network — same 5 people, same cadence,
     confidentiality rule.
•​   One founder-friend on WhatsApp in your timezone and one in India. Different
     jobs: the local one gets your Tuesday; the India one gets your history.
•​   A crisis list: three names you’d actually call at 11pm. Write it down before you
     need it.

Routines that hold up
•​   Recurring community beats events. Weekly cricket league, running club,
     climbing gym, temple/gurdwara volunteering — anything with the same faces
     weekly. Choose one and attend 10 times before judging it.
•​   Exercise is non-negotiable infrastructure, not self-care garnish. 3x/week
     minimum; morning slots survive founder schedules, evenings don’t.
•​   Bound your India hours. Calls to India expand to fill 6am–9am and 8pm–11pm —
     you end up working two timezones and sleeping neither. Fix two windows (e.g.,
     7–8am weekdays, Sunday morning long call) and hold them.
•​   Pre-book festival plans. Find the Diwali event in September, not the week of. FOG
     (Festival of Globe) and India Community Center (Milpitas) run large Bay Area
     events; being anywhere communal on festival days beats a video call from an empty
     apartment.
•​   Fly home on a schedule, not on guilt. Two planned trips a year beats one panic
     trip. Put them in the calendar in January.

Nobody tells you

•​   Sliding scale is a standard, expected ask — therapists build it into their practice.

What vouching means (the fine print)
A vouched entry carries your name. The bar: you attended, used, or bought the thing
within the last 6 months. Not “I heard it’s good.” Not “it was great in 2024.” If your
vouch ages past 6 months, it expires and the entry needs a fresh one. This is the entire
quality mechanism — guard it.
