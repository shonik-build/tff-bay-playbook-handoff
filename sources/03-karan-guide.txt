Sequencing: what to open and when
   • Week 1: apply to the Amex Nova Credit flow and Zolve at the earliest (both need minimal
     hard-inquiry on your credit history)
   • Month 2–3: if both lane 1 and lane 2 were declined, add one secured card (OpenSky if
     you want zero hard-pull risk, Capital One Quicksilver Secured if you already have an
     ITIN).
   • Month 6–9: call the issuer of your oldest secured/newcomer card and ask about
     graduation to an unsecured product or a credit-limit increase. Do not apply for a new card
     yet & ask your current issuer first since it's a soft pull.
   • Month 12+: once you're in the 670–700 band, apply for one everyday-spend card that
     matches your life. Evaluate Chase Freedom (category cash back) or, if you're renting, Bilt
     (points on rent with no transaction fee). This is also when a travel card (Amex
     Gold/Platinum, Chase Sapphire) starts to make sense for the India route.

Utilization and rewards, optimized
Utilization is the single lever you control most precisely.

   • Multiple-card utilization: bureaus look at both per-card and aggregate utilization. One
     maxed card among three still hurts the aggregate number even if the other two are empty.
     Spread spend, or pay a card down mid-cycle.
   • Rewards come second, structure comes first: in year one, optimize for tradeline count
     and on-time payment, not cash-back percentage. A 1% card that reports cleanly for 12
     months is worth more than a 5% card that gets closed or mismanaged.

Traps beyond this list
   1. Foreign transaction fees on US cards used for India spend: most newcomer and
      secured cards charge 2.5–3% FX. If you're regularly paying Indian vendors or traveling
      back, get one no-FX card into the mix.
   2. Credit monitoring subscriptions you don't need: Experian, Credit Karma, and your
      card issuers all offer free score tracking.
   3. Applying for too many cards right before a mortgage or major lease: if you're
      planning to sign a lease or apply for a car loan in the next 3 months, freeze new
      applications since each hard inquiry shaves a few points.
Lesser known facts on credit cards
    • A declined Nova Credit application doesn't burn the lane. You can often reapply after 3–6
      months once your US address and phone history look more established; the model
      weighs more than just the CIBIL pull.
    • Authorized-user status on a US spouse or friend's old card (a real relationship, not a
      rented tradeline) is a legitimate way to inherit account age.
    • Secured-card deposits sometimes take 30–45 days to refund after you close or graduate
      the account.

An ITIN is a tax-filing number only — it confers no work authorization, no Social Security
eligibility, and no Earned Income Tax Credit. Don't confuse it with a path to work status.

The CAA route, in detail
Jargon: CAA (Certified Acceptance Agent) — an IRS-authorized person or firm that verifies
your original passport in person (or via approved video call for some applicants) and returns it to
you immediately, submitting a Certificate of Accuracy with your W-7 instead of you mailing the
IRS your only passport.

    • Find one: the IRS publishes an acceptance-agent list by state/city — search it for the Bay
      Area, or ask your corridor CPA/immigration attorney; many run CAA services as part of
      their practice.
   • What they check: passport validity, visa status, and that the W-7 reason code matches
     your actual situation — a mismatched reason code is one of the most common rejection
     triggers.
   • What they can't skip: the CAA route verifies your documents faster and safer, but the
     IRS's own processing clock (7–11 weeks, up to ~14 in the January–April crush) doesn't
     change. A CAA saves you from losing your passport in the mail, not from waiting.

Common rejection reasons
   1.​ Wrong reason code for the actual situation — e.g., checking the spouse box for a
       founder with US-source LLC income. Match the code to the real relationship to the
       return.
   2.​ Missing or expired supporting documents — the passport must be unexpired and the
       visa/I-94 must match the dates claimed on the return.
   3.​ Return attached without the required exception documentation — treaty-benefit and
       passive-income exceptions each need specific supporting paperwork (the treaty article
       number, the withholding certification), not just a note.
   4.​ Illegible or non-notarized foreign-language documents — any non-English
       supporting document needs a certified translation; the IRS will reject the packet rather
       than ask for a fix.



Nobody tells you — ITIN
   • An ITIN expires if unused on a federal return for 3 consecutive years — if your spouse's
     ITIN goes dormant (e.g., you file separately for a stretch), you may need to renew it
     before your next joint return.
   • Some states' ITIN mortgage-lending programs and a handful of banks treat an ITIN as
     sufficient for account opening even for the primary founder — worth asking your bank
     directly rather than assuming SSN is the only path.
   • A CAA can charge a service fee on top of the (free) IRS filing — shop two or three corridor
     CPA firms; the fee varies more than the service does.


3. Day-to-Day Money Management
Rebuild your budget mental model
Converting your Bangalore or Mumbai budget at the exchange rate undersells how different SF
cost structure actually is. Rent, healthcare, and childcare are the line items that break a
naively-converted budget.
   • Build the budget in three buckets: fixed (rent, insurance, subscriptions),
     variable-necessary (food, transit, utilities), and discretionary. Fixed costs in SF run higher
     as a share of income than in most Indian cities (plan for 50% fixed in year one)
   • Re-budget at month 3, not month 1: your first month's spending is distorted by one-time
     setup costs. Use month 3's actuals as your real baseline.

Bill-pay and subscriptions, set up once
   • Autopay every recurring bill from day one — rent, utilities, phone, insurance. A missed
     payment on a thin file does disproportionate damage; autopay removes the risk of
     forgetting during a jet-lagged first month.
   • Consolidate to one primary checking account for bill-pay even if you keep a Mercury
     business account and a Wise account for India transfers — fragmenting bill-pay across
     three accounts is how a payment gets missed.

Emergency fund: size it for visa risk, not just job-loss risk
The standard 3–6 months of expenses advice understates founder-on-a-visa risk: a visa denial,
RFE, or runway problem can compress your timeline to leave the country. Size the fund to cover
a flight home plus 60 days of US obligations (lease break, final payroll, storage) on top of
ordinary living costs.

1. Business Banking: Choosing your
Stack
The eligibility reality for a non-resident founder
All four of the providers below accept US-incorporated companies; none require you personally
to have an SSN. What actually varies is how they treat a founder who isn't a US resident and
doesn't yet have US revenue or a lead investor.

   • Mercury: the most open of the four. It accepts most US-incorporated businesses with
     reasonable KYC and is the default recommendation for a fresh Delaware C-corp with an
     international founder.
   • Brex: historically the strictest & has generally required VC backing or a meaningful cash
     balance (roughly $50k+) and denies most bootstrapped applicants. Capital One
     completed its acquisition of Brex in April 2026, so expect its onboarding and product mix
     to keep shifting through the rest of the year.
   • Rho: positions itself as the strongest Mercury alternative. One platform for banking, cards,
     bill pay, and treasury, with no minimum balance and no per-user fees.
   • Relay: Its value is multiple named sub-accounts (operating, payroll reserve, tax reserve)
     with per-person debit cards and limits, which is genuinely useful once you have more than
     one person spending company money.

Structuring more than one account
A single Mercury checking account is fine for the first six months. Once payroll and taxes are
real line items, split the operating cash into purpose-built accounts — this is the same discipline
behind a personal three-bucket budget, just at the company level:

   • Operating: day-to-day spend, the account your card and bill-pay draw from.
   • Payroll reserve: funded a pay cycle ahead so a bank hiccup never delays a paycheck.
   • Tax reserve: a running set-aside for payroll tax deposits and your eventual corporate tax
     bill (sized as a percentage of revenue/payroll)
Relay's named sub-accounts are the simplest way to do this without opening accounts at
multiple banks; Mercury and Rho both support multiple accounts under one login as well.

Treasury: making idle cash actually earn something
Jargon: Sweep — an automated rule that moves any balance above a threshold you set (your
"liquidity buffer") out of checking and into a higher-yield product, then back when checking dips
below the buffer.

Once you're holding a meaningful runway in the bank, idle cash sitting in a 0% checking account
is a real opportunity cost. Mercury Treasury is the most common product founders reach for
since it generally wants $250k+ in the account before it's worth setting up; the highest
advertised net yields (mid-single digits) apply at much larger deposit sizes, not the entry tier.

4. Intercompany & Cross-Border Banking
(India Entity ↔ US Entity)
Once you've flipped to a Delaware structure with an Indian operating subsidiary underneath, or
you're funding the US entity from India-side resources, the banking questions stop being about
which app to use and start being about FEMA/ODI compliance.

Funding the US entity from the Indian side: ODI
Jargon: ODI (Overseas Direct Investment) is RBI's framework under FEMA governing how an
Indian company invests in or funds a foreign entity as opposed to LRS, which governs an
individual resident's outbound remittances.

   • Route: fund transfers from the Indian parent to the US subsidiary must go through an
     Authorized Dealer (AD) bank and be reported as ODI. You cannot simply wire money
     from an Indian company account to the US entity without this filing.
   • Cap: ODI investment is capped at 400% of the Indian parent's net worth under the
     automatic route; anything beyond that needs RBI approval.
   • Structure limit: current rules permit round-trip structures (money that in substance
     returns to India-linked ownership) on the automatic route only if the structure doesn't
     exceed two layers of subsidiaries — a detail worth checking with counsel before adding a
     holding-company layer.
   • 2026 tightening: FEMA (Non-Debt Instruments) Amendment Rules effective May 2026
     add a PMLA-aligned beneficial-ownership test to the inbound leg of round-trip structures,
     and require government approval where a beneficial owner holds 10%+ in a
     land-border-country entity within the structure. If your structure has any layer touching a
     land-border-country entity, get this checked explicitly.

Intercompany transactions once both entities exist
Once the US subsidiary and Indian parent (or vice versa, depending on which way the flip runs)
both exist, every transaction between them — intercompany loans, service fees, royalty
payments, supply arrangements — is transfer-pricing territory:

   • Arm's-length requirement: Sections 92–92F of India's Income Tax Act require these
     transactions to be priced as if the two entities were unrelated parties. This isn't optional
     paperwork — India's Transfer Pricing Officer can and does scrutinize intercompany flows
     during assessment.
   • Documentation: keep a contemporaneous transfer-pricing study or at minimum a
     documented rationale for how intercompany fees/royalties were priced — reconstructing
     this after the fact during an audit is far harder than building it as you go.
  • Banking practicality: route intercompany payments through the AD bank relationship set
    up for ODI reporting, not through a personal or ad hoc business account — the paper trail
    matters as much as the transaction itself.

Practical setup: which bank talks to which
  • On the Indian side: your existing relationship bank (ICICI, HDFC, Axis, SBI) typically acts
    as the AD bank for ODI filings — confirm they have an active AD-I license and a team
    that handles ODI, not every branch does this routinely.
  • On the US side: Mercury, Rho, or a similar business account receives the funds; nothing
    about the US-side account opening changes because the money originated as an
    ODI-compliant transfer, but keep the ODI filing reference number attached to the
    incoming-wire records for your own audit trail.
  • Get both sides' advisors talking to each other: the Indian CA/company secretary
    handling ODI filings and the US-side accountant handling the receiving entity's books
    should reconcile the same transaction — this is the single most common place where the
    two records quietly diverge.

Do this now — intercompany banking
  • ☐ Confirm your Indian bank's AD-I status and ODI-filing experience before relying
    on them for the transfer — not every branch handles this routinely
  • ☐ Get a transfer-pricing rationale documented before the first intercompany
    invoice, not after — contemporaneous documentation is far stronger than reconstructed
    documentation
  • ☐ Check any land-border-country entity in your structure against the 2026
    beneficial-ownership rule
  • ☐ Keep the ODI filing reference attached to every related US-side bank record — for
    reconciliation and any future audit

Lesser known facts on intercompany banking
  • ODI reporting delays are common when the AD bank's ODI team is unfamiliar with a
    startup-flip structure specifically (as opposed to a traditional outbound acquisition). Ask
    directly whether they've processed a founder-flip ODI filing before.
  • The 400% net worth cap is measured against the Indian parent's net worth, which for an
    early-stage flip structure may be small. This can meaningfully constrain how much the
    Indian entity can fund the US side directly, pushing founders toward personal LRS
    remittances or US-side fundraising instead.
  • Two separate compliance clocks run in parallel: India's ODI/FEMA reporting and the US
    entity's own tax and banking KYC and they rarely have the same deadlines. A shared
    calendar between both advisors avoids the trap of one side quietly falling behind.

1. The 60-Day Window: Mechanics,
Deadlines, and What Happens If You Miss
It
What starts the clock
Jargon: QLE (Qualifying Life Event) is a defined life change: moving to California, marriage,
birth, loss of other coverage, etc. that opens a Special Enrollment Period (SEP) outside the
normal Nov–Jan open-enrollment window.

"Permanently moved to/within California" is itself a listed QLE. The clock starts on your move
date. Covered California's system checks this: if the date you enter is more than 60 days in the
past, the application flags it and you may not qualify for the Special Enrollment Period at all.

If you miss it
Missing the 60-day window doesn't mean no coverage until November. It means no Covered
California marketplace plan until the next open enrollment (typically Nov–Jan for a Jan 1 start),
unless another QLE happens first (a job that offers a group plan, marriage, a child). Your
practical options in the gap:

   • Extend the bridge plan: Insubuy/VisitorsCoverage-style visitor medical plans are
     month-to-month and can be renewed past the original gap if needed (expensive relative
     to a subsidized marketplace plan, but real coverage)
   • Short-term medical insurance: US-licensed short-term plans, sold for up to 12 months
     in California with restrictions, cover unexpected illness/injury but exclude pre-existing
     conditions and preventive care, and are not required to comply with the No Surprises Act.
   • Push for the group plan: if your startup is raising or hiring, a group health plan through
     the company sidesteps the individual-market SEP question entirely and typically has no
     such 60-day trap for new hires (group plans have their own, usually more forgiving,
     enrollment rules).

Do this now: the 60-day window
   • ☐ Enter your move date correctly the first time: the system calculates your 60 days
     from what you enter, not from when you notice the deadline
   • ☐ Gather proof-of-prior-coverage documentation before you need it: a letter or
     record showing coverage in the 60 days before your move
   • ☐ Enroll by day 30: coverage starts the 1st of next month regardless of when in the
     window you enroll, so earlier enrollment shortens your bridge-plan period
   • ☐ If you're going to miss it, start a short-term or extended bridge plan before day
     60, not after (you want zero-day gaps between coverage types)

The 60-day window constraints
   • The proof-of-prior-coverage requirement trips up founders who assume their Indian
     insurance (or none at all) is irrelevant to a US move. Bring whatever documentation you
     have (even a bridge-plan policy document from the weeks right after landing)
   • A dependent spouse or child added mid-window has their own clock tied to the same
     QLE: Don't assume they're automatically covered by your enrollment; confirm each family
     member is explicitly added to the application.
2. Plans Without an Employer

The 2026 subsidy cliff, in plain terms
Two changes make 2026 a materially worse year to be uninsured than 2025 was:

    • Enhanced federal subsidies expired Dec 31, 2025. Statewide average post-subsidy
      premiums jumped from roughly $151/month (2025) to a projected ~$261/month (2026).
    • The subsidy cliff is now a cliff, not a slope: federal premium tax credits are available
      only up to 400% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL); projected at roughly $63,000/year for
      a single person in 2026. Earn $1 over that line and you lose 100% of the federal subsidy
      at once, not gradually. A real-world example from 2026 coverage: a person's premium
      roughly doubles, from about $554/month to over $1,000/month, the moment they cross
      the line.
    • California's own backstop: the state allocated its own subsidy program, but it targets
      households up to 165% FPL; it doesn't help the 250–450% FPL range where many
      funded founders paying themselves a modest but real salary actually sit.
The practical implication from Article 8 still holds and matters more this year: your declared
founder salary is a healthcare-cost decision, not just a payroll decision. Model the premium at
your actual planned income before you set salary, ideally with your accountant in the room.

The triage rule, restated
   • Telehealth first, always, when it's not urgent: a same-day video visit ($34–37, no
     insurance required) is enough for most infections, rashes, prescription refills, and "is this
     serious" questions — and is faster than a new-patient PCP slot in your first months.
   • Urgent care for anything that needs in-person hands: stitches, sprains, fevers that
     need a look, suspected infections, X-ray-needing injuries.
   • ER only for: chest pain, difficulty breathing, major trauma, stroke symptoms, severe
     allergic reaction. If you're unsure whether something is ER-level, call the telehealth line
     first unless it's obviously life-threatening. Most triage calls are free or near-free and can
     direct you correctly in minutes.

Billing protections if you do end up in the ER
The No Surprises Act (federal law) directly protects you in the emergency scenario:

   • Emergency care is protected regardless of network: if you end up at an out-of-network
     ER, you cannot legally be charged more than your plan's in-network cost-sharing amount
     for most emergency services. This applies even without prior authorization, because
     emergencies don't wait for authorization.
  • What's NOT protected: ground ambulance rides , any out-of-network care you knowingly
    and willingly consent to, and non-emergency care you choose to get at an out-of-network
    facility.
  • Which plans this applies to: most private insurance, including Covered California
    marketplace plans and employer plans. It generally does NOT apply to short-term medical
    plans or bridge/visitor insurance.
  • If you get billed anyway: you can dispute it by calling the CMS No Surprises Help Desk
     or your state insurance department; incorrectly balanced-billed amounts get corrected
     more often than founders expect once formally disputed.

Do this now: ER and billing
  • ☐ Save a telehealth app (Sesame or similar) on your phone before you need it
  • ☐ Know your plan's in-network ER and urgent care options before an emergency
  • ☐ If you get an ER bill that looks like balance billing, dispute it
  • ☐ Keep your insurance card (physical or photo) on you at all times

Nobody tells you: ER and billing
  • Hospitals are required to have publicly posted price lists ("price transparency" files) but
    they're often difficult to actually parse; a faster path is calling the hospital's billing
    department directly and asking for the specific CPT-code price for your situation before a
    non-emergency procedure.
  • An ER bill arriving weeks later with a much larger number than expected is common even
    when nothing went wrong; hospitals bill in stages (facility fee, physician fee, lab fee arrive
    separately). Don't panic or pay immediately; wait to see the full set of bills and check them
    against your insurer's Explanation of Benefits before paying anything.

The conversion mechanics, in order
    1. Your existing resident savings account can only convert to NRO (you cannot convert a
       resident account into NRE)
    2. Open a fresh NRE account if you plan to remit US income into India and want it tax-free

    3. Most major banks (ICICI, HDFC, SBI, Axis) accept the conversion request with a visa

The NRO repatriation paperwork, worked
Moving money out of an NRO account (say, to fund your US life or pay yourself back) isn't a
simple wire:

    • Under ₹5 lakh in the financial year: file Form 15CA Part A yourself — no CA certificate
      needed.
    • Above ₹5 lakh in the financial year: get Form 15CB from a Chartered Accountant
      certifying taxes are settled, then file Form 15CA Part C.
    • Form renaming, not a substantive change: for remittances made on or after April 1,
      2026, Form 15CA is renamed Form 145 and Form 15CB becomes Form 146 under the
        new Income-tax Act, 2025; the ₹5 lakh threshold and CA-certification requirement carry

        old form names.

LRS (Liberalised Remittance Scheme) is RBI's rule letting each Indian resident individual send
up to $250,000 per financial year abroad for permitted purposes.

   • The cap: $250,000 per financial year, per resident individual: relevant when parents are
     funding you, or you're moving your own resident-held money before converting to NRI
     status.
   • TCS (Tax Collected at Source): kicks in above ₹10 lakh/year in remittances. As of the
     2026 Budget: education and medical remittances are taxed at a reduced 2% (down from
     5%); overseas tour packages at 2% with no minimum threshold; everything else
     (including funding a child's living costs or a startup) still attracts 20% TCS.
   • TCS is not a net cost: it's collected upfront and claimable back through the sender's
     Indian income tax return (ITR) but it's still a real cash-flow hit in the year it's collected,
     since the credit doesn't arrive until the return is processed.

3. Supporting Family — The Practical
Mechanics
Direction 1: you supporting parents in India
   • India side: money you send to parents is a gift from a child to a "relative" under Indian tax
      law is tax-free for them, in any amount, with no reporting requirement on the Indian side.
   • US side (Form 709): gifts above the annual exclusion ($19,000 per recipient in 2026)
     require you to file IRS Form 709.
   • Married founders: you and your spouse can jointly give up to $38,000 per recipient per
     year by electing to "gift-split" on Form 709; useful if you're supporting both parents or
     sending a larger one-time amount.

Direction 2: parents (or other relatives) funding you
   • Not taxable income: a genuine gift from a foreign parent is not included in your US gross
     income
   • Form 3520 disclosure threshold: you must report foreign gifts on Form 3520 once the
     aggregate from all foreign persons tied to one source exceeds $100,000 in the tax year:
     both parents' gifts count together toward this threshold, and once you're over it, every
     individual gift above $5,000 must be separately itemized.
   • Filing logistics: Form 3520 must be mailed separately to the IRS's Ogden, Utah office —
     it cannot be e-filed with your regular return, even though the deadline (April 15,
     extendable to October 15) matches your 1040.
   • On the India side: parents funding you from India route through LRS (Section 2) and face
     TCS above ₹10 lakh/year


4. Other NRI Banking Loose Ends
FCNR deposits: parking foreign currency in India
FCNR (Foreign Currency Non-Resident) deposit is a fixed deposit held in a foreign currency
(USD, GBP, EUR, and others) at an Indian bank. The principal and interest stay in that foreign
currency, so you carry zero rupee exchange-rate risk on the deposit itself.

   • Rates: USD FCNR rates commonly run 4.5–5.2% depending on bank and tenure as of
     2026, with some banks (RBL, YES Bank) advertising up to ~4.85% on 1–2 year USD
     deposits can meaningfully competitive with US savings rates and without INR conversion
     risk.
   • India tax treatment: interest is completely exempt from Indian income tax for NRIs.
  • US tax treatment: that interest is still part of your US taxable income once you're a US
    tax resident
  • Use case: a reasonable place to hold USD you don't need in the US near-term but want
    to keep accessible for India-side plans (family support, a future property purchase)
    without INR conversion risk in the meantime.

PPF: continue, don't ignore
  • Notify within a month: tell your PPF bank or post office of your NRI status; the account
    itself continues to maturity but cannot be renewed after that.
  • Calendar the maturity date: an NRI cannot open a new PPF account or extend an
    existing one past maturity, so plan for that account's closure rather than assuming it
    continues indefinitely.

Mutual funds, demat, and the PFIC problem
  • Update KYC to NRI status on every mutual fund folio and your demat/trading account —
    this is a compliance requirement, not optional paperwork.
  • The PFIC trap: Indian mutual funds are taxed punitively under US PFIC (Passive Foreign
    Investment Company) rules once you're a US tax resident, often at top rates plus interest
    charges on deemed gains. Most NRIs exit Indian mutual funds before their first full US tax
    year, rather than continuing to invest in them.
  • Get a PFIC-aware CA before adding new money to any Indian mutual fund. This is one
    of the most expensive mistakes in the whole corridor if it's discovered after the fact rather
    than planned around in advance.

Do this now — NRI banking
  • ☐ Compare FCNR rates across 2–3 banks before parking a large USD sum
  • ☐ Notify your PPF provider of NRI status within a month
  • ☐ Update KYC to NRI on every mutual fund and demat account
  • ☐ Talk to a PFIC-aware CA before adding money to any Indian mutual fund
