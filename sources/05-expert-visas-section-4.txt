4. Visas for Indian Founders
Stage: Before you fly · Type: Guide · Draft v1

What this page is for: Picking the right visa path before you book flights. Most Indian founders land on
one of four: a B-1 to scout, an O-1A to build, an L-1A if you already run a real company in India, or an
H-1B if you already hold one.

Jargon: A petition is the application a US company files with USCIS asking to employ you. You cannot
file for yourself directly, but a company you own can petition for you.

B-1: scout, don't work

The B-1 lets you take meetings, negotiate, and talk to investors. It does not let you write production code
for your US entity, sign as a US employee, or draw US salary. Border officers have tightened scrutiny of
B-1 entries since 2025, so carry a return ticket, a meeting schedule, and proof of Indian ties.

The real problem is the interview queue. First-time B1/B2 interview waits at Indian consulates ran
roughly 100 to 400 days across 2025 and 2026 depending on the city, with Chennai and Hyderabad often
shorter than Mumbai and Delhi. If you already hold a valid B1/B2 from a past trip, you have dodged this
entirely. Check current waits on the State Department's per-city tool before planning, because these
numbers move monthly.

O-1A: the founder default

The O-1A is for "extraordinary ability" in business or STEM. That sounds intimidating. In practice it is an
evidence-assembly exercise. You must hit 3 of 8 USCIS criteria, codified at 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii),
unless you hold one major internationally recognised award, which almost nobody does:

1. Awards · 2. Selective memberships · 3. Press about you · 4. Judging others' work · 5. Original
contributions of major significance · 6. Scholarly articles · 7. Critical role at distinguished organizations ·
8. High remuneration.

Funded founders most commonly clear: critical role (founder or CTO of a VC-backed company), high
remuneration (Indian salary benchmarked against Indian norms counts), judging (hackathon or
accelerator judging, peer review), press (named coverage in real publications), and original
contributions (patents, widely adopted product). VC funding from recognised investors helps under
awards and critical roles. Start collecting evidence months before you engage a lawyer.

One requirement nobody plans for: every O-1 petition needs a written advisory opinion from a peer
group, labor organization or expert in your field, under 8 CFR 214.2(o)(5). It is waived only where no
appropriate body exists. Turnaround runs from a couple of days to a few weeks, so start it early rather
than at filing.

Numbers as of mid-2026 (verify current):

    ●​ Approval rate roughly 94% recently, RFE rate roughly 18.7%. Jargon: An RFE (Request for
       Evidence) is USCIS asking for more proof. It adds 2 to 4 months and usually still ends in
       approval.
    ●​ Government fees: the I-129 fee for O classification is $1,055, or $530 if your company has 25 or
       fewer full-time-equivalent employees, plus an asylum program fee of $600, or $300 for those
       same small employers. A startup sponsoring its own founder therefore pays about $830, not the
       H-1B or L-1 numbers often quoted.
    ●​ Attorney fees run $6,000 to $15,000. Premium processing is $2,965, effective 1 March 2026, for
       a 15 business day decision. All in, most founder cases land between $10,000 and $19,000.
    ●​ Timeline: about 2 months from engagement to approval when your evidence is already
       collected, roughly a month of petition prep plus 15 business days with premium processing. Prep
       is the variable, not USCIS. If you are still chasing press, letters or judging invites, add however
       long that takes. Consulate stamping in India is a separate queue on top.
    ●​ Duration: up to 3 years initially, then extensions in increments of up to 1 year, with no overall
       cap. Spouses get O-3, which carries no work authorization, so plan for it.

Why O-1A beats waiting on H-1B: no lottery, no annual cap, and critically no per-country cap, the
thing that puts India-born applicants in decade-long employment green card queues.
L-1A: if you already run a company in India

If your Indian company is real and staffed, you can transfer yourself into a US entity instead of proving
you are extraordinary. The test is structural rather than reputational. You need a qualifying relationship
between the Indian company and the US one, meaning parent, subsidiary, affiliate or branch under
common ownership and control, plus one continuous year of managerial or executive work at the Indian
entity in the three years before filing. Your evidence is payroll, org charts and contracts rather than press
clippings.

The catch is the new office rule. If the US entity is newly formed, USCIS approves you for one year, not
three, and you spend that year proving you built something real. Officers also expect secured physical
premises, usually a signed commercial lease with room for the headcount in your business plan. Virtual
offices and coworking desks draw heavy scrutiny. At extension you are judged on execution, not on the
plan you filed.

Timelines are shorter than the O-1A, about a month with premium processing, because the evidence
already exists in your company records. You are producing payroll, incorporation papers, org charts and a
lease rather than building a case for your own significance. Costs sit close to the O-1A. The I-129 fee for
L classification is $1,385, or $695 for small employers, plus the same asylum program fee and a $500
fraud prevention fee on new L-1 petitions. Premium processing is the same $2,965. Approval rates run a
little lower and RFE rates higher than the O-1A, because new office cases get read hard. Price both routes
before you assume the O-1A is your only option.

H-1B: the founder rule changed in 2025

If you already hold an H-1B, your Delaware C-corp can file a transfer with no new lottery. The bigger
change is for owners. Since 17 January 2025, USCIS has removed the old employer-employee control test
and expressly allows a founder with a controlling interest to be sponsored by their own company,
provided you spend the majority of your time on specialty occupation work rather than running the
business. The trade-off is validity: initial approval and first extension are capped at 18 months each,
against three years for a standard H-1B. You no longer need outside directors to make this work, which
was true before 2025 and is repeated in a lot of stale advice.

Think green card from day one

The O-1A is temporary. The long-term play is EB-1A, the green card cousin of the O-1A with a higher
evidence bar. EB-1 India is badly backlogged: the September 2026 Final Action Date is 15 October 2022,
and the State Department has warned EB-1 India may go unavailable entirely before the fiscal year ends
on 30 September 2026, with new numbers from 1 October. Realistic I-140 to green card timelines for
India run roughly 4 to 6 years. File your I-140 as early as your evidence supports it. Filing locks your
priority date, your place in the queue, and every month you delay is a month added at the back end.

If you came in on L-1A, your green card cousin is EB-1C, not EB-1A. It asks for a genuine multinational
manager or executive role instead of extraordinary ability, which is an easier story for some founders to
tell, though the US entity must have been doing business for at least a year before you can file. It sits in
the same backlogged EB-1 queue, so the filing-early logic applies identically.

Do this now

    ●​ Check your existing B1/B2. If valid, your scouting trip needs zero new paperwork
    ●​ Start an O-1 evidence folder today: press links, judging invites, funding docs, salary proof,
       patents
    ●​ Get 2 to 3 quotes from founder-focused immigration lawyers ($6,000 to $15,000 range, and
       ask which 3 criteria they would argue)
    ●​ Check current India consulate wait times on the State Department site before booking anything
    ●​ Ask your lawyer about EB-1A timing in the same engagement, because the priority date
       compounds
    ●​ If you already run an Indian company with staff, price the L-1A alongside the O-1A. The
       entity structure has to be right before either route works, so ask before you incorporate, not after

Nobody tells you

    ●​ Your own startup can sponsor your O-1. The "employer" can be a US entity you majority-own,
       though many lawyers add board oversight to strengthen the petition. This is why the Delaware
       entity (article 5) usually comes before the visa.
    ●​ Judging is the cheapest criterion to build. Accepting hackathon judge and pitch competition jury
       invitations over 3 to 6 months can manufacture a whole criterion from scratch.
    ●​ The 94% approval rate is survivorship-shaped. Lawyers filter out weak cases before filing, so if a
       good lawyer hesitates on your case, that is signal, not gatekeeping.
    ●​ On a new office L-1A, the one year clock starts when you land, not when you file. Founders who
       spend the first quarter on setup instead of hiring reach the extension with nothing to show, and the
       extension is judged on what you actually built.
