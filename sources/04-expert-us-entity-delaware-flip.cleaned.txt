5. US Entity & the Delaware Flip
Stage: Before you fly · Type: Guide · Draft v1 — July 2026
What this page is for: To decide whether you need a US entity, which structure to pick, and how the Indian-side rules (FEMA/ODI) constrain you. Most founders get this wrong and unwinding it can cost lakhs and get it very wrong, and unwinding it can cost crores.

The decision rule: The decision rule is simple: if your primary goal is to raise capital from US venture capital firms, you will likely need a Delaware parent company, as most US funds (including YC) prefer not to invest in an Indian parent entity. However, if your goal is simply to sell to US customers, incorporating in Delaware is not necessary. An Indian company, or an Indian company with a US subsidiary, can invoice and serve US customers without issue. In short: structure your company for fundraising, not for customers.
Jargon: A Delaware flip means founders and existing shareholders swap their Indian-company shares for shares in a new Delaware C-corp, making the Indian company a subsidiary.
The Indian-side rules: ODI 2022 and round-tripping
The Overseas Investment (OI) Rules, 2022 (issued August 2022) govern Indians acquiring foreign shares. Two things matter for a flip:
	•	Round-tripping is now permitted for bona fide business — the old blanket ban on structures where a foreign entity owns an Indian one (money “rounding” back to India) is gone, but only up to two layers of subsidiaries and only where the US parent has genuine substance: real US customers, US operations, a documented commercial rationale. A shell Delaware parent existing purely to hold Indian shares invites RBI trouble.
	•	Your share swap uses your LRS headroom. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme caps each resident Indian at $250,000 per financial year for overseas investment. Acquiring US parent shares (by swap or purchase) is an ODI transaction under these rules, valued by a SEBI-registered merchant banker. Budget the valuation report at ₹1–2 lakh and FEMA/RBI filings (Form FC, annual APR) at ₹1–3 lakh in professional fees.
Jargon: FEMA is India’s Foreign Exchange Management Act — the law behind all of this. ODI is Overseas Direct Investment — an Indian resident buying into a foreign company.

The warning label: reverse flips are brutal
In 2024–25, Meesho, Groww and Razorpay collectively paid $600M+ in US taxes to un-flip and move HQ back to India for domestic IPOs (Meesho ~$288M; Groww ~$160M). If your realistic exit is an India listing, think hard before flipping at all. The flip is close to a one-way door once value builds up in the Delaware parent.

	•	☐ Answer one question honestly: are US VCs going to lead your next round? If no, don’t flip yet

Nobody tells you
	•	YC and most US funds will pay for or heavily subsidize the flip paperwork post-acceptance — don’t pre-emptively spend $25k flipping “to be ready” before a term sheet or acceptance exists.
	•	The two-layer rule bites later: Delaware parent → Indian subsidiary is fine, but add a Singapore entity or a second step-down and you may need prior RBI approval. Keep the stack flat.
	•	Transfer pricing between your Indian subsidiary (now a cost center doing R&D) and the US parent needs an arm’s-length agreement from day one — Indian tax officers audit flipped structures specifically for this. Cheap to set up, expensive to retrofit.

ESOPs during US Entity & the Delaware Flip

Your ESOP pool and the flip
Options are not shares. This is the sentence that catches most founders out. When you do a share swap, shareholders swap; optionholders do not, because they hold a contract, not a security. Your ESOP does not travel with the flip automatically. It has to be moved, deliberately, by paperwork, and every unvested and vested option in the pool needs to be accounted for.

Shares already issued to employees who exercised early are a separate problem. Those people are shareholders. They participate in the swap, which means each of them becomes an ODI filer with their own LRS headroom, their own valuation compliance and their own Form FC. Twelve employees who exercised is twelve extra sets of FEMA filings. Keep the pre-flip cap table as thin as you can.

Whichever route you take, preserve the original vesting commencement date in writing. If the regrant paperwork resets the clock, an employee three years into a four-year vest starts again, and you will find out about it in the worst possible way.

Jargon: Vesting commencement date is the date the vesting clock starts, usually the joining date, and it is often earlier than the grant date. It is the number that actually matters in a regrant.
Size the pool while the cap table is open
US funds expect a 10 to 15 percent unallocated pool post-money at seed. Indian norms sit closer to 5 to 10 percent. If you flip with an Indian-sized pool, your first US term sheet will demand a top-up, and that top-up will come entirely out of founder and existing investor dilution. Expand the pool as part of the flip, not after the term sheet arrives.
The tax split, which nobody models in advance
After the flip your Indian employees hold options over shares of a foreign company. That changes three things.

Exercise is still taxed in India as a perquisite under section 17(2)(vi), on FMV minus exercise price, as salary income, with TDS withheld by the Indian subsidiary. The FMV is now that of an unlisted foreign share, which means a Category I merchant banker valuation on every exercise date.

The DPIIT deferral quietly disappears. The section 192(1C) benefit that lets eligible startups defer ESOP TDS by up to 48 months depends on an 80-IAC certificate. A subsidiary of a foreign parent will not hold one. If your team was counting on that deferral, tell them before the flip, not after.

Every employee holding parent shares must report them in Schedule FA of their income tax return as a foreign asset. Non-disclosure is a Black Money Act exposure with a ₹10 lakh penalty, and the employee carries it personally. Build the Schedule FA reminder into your annual HR calendar. On sale, gains on unlisted foreign shares are long-term after 24 months and taxed at 12.5 percent without indexation.

One more, on the US side: ISOs are only meaningful to US taxpayers. Your India team gets NSOs regardless of what the plan document permits, and the ISO tax advantage your US counsel talks about is worth nothing to them.

Jargon: NSO is a non-qualified stock option, the default option type. ISO is an incentive stock option, which carries a US tax benefit and only helps people who file US taxes.

Nobody tells you
	•	Dematerialisation is on the critical path and takes 6 to 8 weeks. A private company that is not a small company cannot transfer shares in physical form, and a flip is a transfer of every share on the register. Founders discover this in week two of a three-month timeline and lose a month.
	•	The ESOP expense does not vanish when the plan moves to Delaware. The Indian subsidiary still books the Ind AS 102 charge for its own employees, usually via a recharge from the parent, and that recharge is a related-party transaction that lands squarely in the transfer pricing agreement mentioned above. Get it into the same document rather than discovering it at the first post-flip audit.
	•	Cashless exercise mechanics change after the flip. An Indian employee paying cash to acquire foreign shares runs into the OI Rules, where employee share schemes offered globally on a uniform basis sit under a specific carve-out in Schedule III. Whether a given exercise consumes that employee's LRS headroom depends on how the exercise is structured. Get your CA to confirm the mechanics before you promise anyone a cashless window.
